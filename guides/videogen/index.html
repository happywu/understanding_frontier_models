2020-2022: U-Net Diffusion Era

DDPM, LDM (Stable Diffusion) proved latent diffusion works for images. U-Net backbone, discrete noise schedules, hundreds of sampling steps.

DiT paper (Peebles & Xie) replaced U-Net with ViT. Flow matching (Lipman et al.) introduced straight ODE paths. These two innovations converge.

2024: Video Generation Explosion

Sora, CogVideoX, HunyuanVideo, Movie Gen, Stable Video Diffusion. 3D VAEs, spacetime patches, 13B+ parameter models. Flow matching becomes dominant.

2025: Scale + Alignment

Step by Step

1

Encode Text

The text prompt is encoded into a sequence of embedding vectors using a pre-trained text encoder (T5-XXL, CLIP, or an MLLM). These embeddings capture the semantic meaning of the prompt and will guide the generation process.

Key insight: Different encoders have complementary strengths. CLIP provides aligned image-text embeddings (good for global semantics), while T5 provides rich token-level representations (good for compositional details). Many models use both.

2

Denoise with DiT

Starting from pure Gaussian noise in the latent space, the Diffusion Transformer iteratively refines the latent representation. At each step, it predicts a velocity field (flow matching) or noise (DDPM) conditioned on the text embeddings and current timestep.

Key insight: The DiT operates on spacetime patches — 3D tokens that span both space and time. This allows a single transformer to jointly model spatial composition and temporal dynamics.

3

Decode Latent to Pixels

The clean latent z1 is decoded back to pixel space by the VAE decoder. This involves upsampling in both spatial and temporal dimensions using transposed 3D convolutions.

Key insight: The VAE was pre-trained separately to achieve high-fidelity reconstruction. A 4×8×8 compression ratio means the DiT only needs to operate on a representation that is 256× smaller than the original video.

Why Latent Space?

📈

Computational Cost Comparison

For a 5-second, 720p, 24fps video:

Pixel space: 120 frames × 1280 × 720 × 3 = 332M values Latent space: 30 × 160 × 90 × 16 = 6.9M values Ratio: ~48× fewer values to denoise

Self-attention in the transformer scales as O(n²). Reducing the sequence length by 48× reduces attention cost by ~2,300×. This is why all frontier video models operate in latent space.

CFG Doubles Compute

Because CFG requires two forward passes per step, it doubles inference cost. Recent innovations like CFG-Zero* (2025) optimize the guidance scaling and zero out early ODE steps where velocity estimation is most inaccurate, improving quality without extra cost.

3D VAE Tokenization

Before the diffusion model can work its magic, raw video must be compressed into a compact latent representation. This is the job of the 3D Variational Autoencoder.

Why Compress?

A single second of 720p video contains ~71 million pixel values. Running diffusion directly in pixel space would require attention over millions of tokens — computationally impossible. The VAE compresses this by 256× spatially (4× temporal, 8× in each spatial dimension), making diffusion tractable.

From 2D to 3D: The Evolution

Early latent diffusion (Stable Diffusion, SDXL) used a 2D spatial VAE: each frame is independently encoded into a 2D latent, then the model generates frame-by-frame. This works for images but fails for video — there is no temporal compression, adjacent frames have no latent-level coherence, and the result is flickering, temporally inconsistent output.

2D Spatial VAE

Compresses each frame independently: H×W×3 → H/8×W/8×4. No temporal awareness. Used in SD 1.x, SDXL, DALL-E.

Problem for video: No temporal compression. Adjacent frames are encoded independently, causing flicker and temporal inconsistency.

SD 1.5, SDXL, Imagen

3D Spatiotemporal VAE

Compresses video jointly in space and time: T×H×W×3 → T/4×H/8×W/8×16. Temporal features are entangled in the latent — the diffusion model sees a temporally coherent representation.

Key models: MagViT-v2 (Google, tokenizes video into discrete codes), Cosmos Tokenizer (NVIDIA, continuous + discrete), Wan-VAE (Alibaba, unlimited-length), CogVideoX VAE.

All 2024–2025 frontier video models

⚠

Why 2D VAE Fails for Video

If you encode each frame independently with a 2D VAE, the latent codes for adjacent frames have no guaranteed smoothness. The diffusion model must learn temporal coherence entirely on its own, which is extremely difficult. 3D VAEs solve this by compressing the temporal dimension (typically 4×), so the latent space is already temporally smooth. This is why every serious video model post-2023 uses a 3D VAE.

VAE Training Objective

The VAE is trained with a multi-objective loss:

  • L1 reconstruction: Pixel-level fidelity between input and output
  • LPIPS: Perceptual similarity via deep features (VGG)
  • GAN: Adversarial loss from a PatchGAN discriminator for sharpness
  • KL divergence: Regularizes latent distribution toward N(0, I) so the diffusion model can sample from it

Flow Matching

Flow matching is the training objective that replaced DDPM for frontier video models. Instead of learning to predict and remove noise step-by-step, it learns a continuous velocity field along straight-line paths.

DDPM vs Flow Matching

DDPM (Traditional)

  • Discrete timesteps (T = 1000)
  • Learns noise prediction: εθ(xt, t)
  • Requires noise schedule design
  • ~50–250 sampling steps
  • Curved, stochastic paths

Flow Matching (Modern)

  • Continuous time t ∈ [0, 1]
  • Learns velocity field: vθ(xt, t)
  • Schedule-free (straight paths)
  • ~25–50 sampling steps
  • Straight optimal-transport paths

The Core Idea

Flow matching constructs a time-dependent probability path pt(x) that smoothly interpolates between noise (t=0) and data (t=1). A neural network learns the velocity field vθ that generates this flow.

💡 Intuition: Think of it as transportation

Imagine a fleet of particles starting at random positions (noise) that need to reach specific destinations (data). DDPM gives them wandering, curved routes. Flow matching gives them straight-line highways — the shortest possible paths (optimal transport). Shorter paths = easier to learn = fewer steps to travel.

Reflow: Straightening Trajectories Further

💡 Why Straight Paths Matter

Even with flow matching, the learned velocity field isn't perfectly straight — the model approximates. Reflow (Liu et al., 2023) is a technique to iteratively straighten these paths:

  1. Train a flow matching model normally (pass 1)
  2. Use the trained model to generate (noise, data) pairs by flowing noise to data
  3. Re-train on these paired samples — the new coupling is straighter because the pairs are already matched
  4. Repeat. Each iteration produces straighter paths.

Result: After reflowing, you can sample in as few as 1–4 steps with minimal quality loss (vs. 25–50 steps for first-pass models). This is the theoretical foundation behind distillation techniques used in production (e.g., FLUX-Schnell, InstaFlow).

The DiT Block

adaLN-Zero DiT Block (following Peebles & Xie, 2023)

💡 Key Innovation: adaLN-Zero

Instead of cross-attention for conditioning (like U-Net diffusion), DiT uses Adaptive Layer Normalization. The timestep t and text conditioning c are combined and projected into scale (γ) and shift (β) parameters that modulate the LayerNorm in each block. The "Zero" variant initializes the final linear layer to zero, so each block starts as an identity function — this stabilizes training significantly.

Why Dual/Triple Encoders?

💡 Complementary Strengths

CLIP is trained contrastively on image-text pairs, so it produces embeddings that are naturally aligned between modalities. But its text representation is coarse (77-token limit) and misses compositional details.

T5 is a language model with bidirectional attention, producing rich token-level representations that capture complex relationships and long descriptions. But it has no visual alignment.

Using both gives you: global visual alignment (CLIP) + detailed semantic understanding (T5). Movie Gen goes further with a triple encoder adding character-level precision (ByT5) for accurate text rendering in video.

Generation Tasks

Modern video generation systems support a family of related tasks beyond simple text-to-video. Understanding these tasks—and how they relate architecturally—is essential for building a complete system.

Core Generation Tasks

Task Landscape

T2V: Text-to-Video

The foundational task. A text prompt is encoded via T5/CLIP, the DiT denoises from Gaussian noise conditioned on text, and the VAE decodes the latent to pixels. All frontier models support this as the primary task.

I2V: Image-to-Video

Animate a static reference image into a video. Three architectural approaches:

Channel Concat

Encode the first frame through VAE into latent z1. Concatenate channel-wise with noise tensor. DiT input channels expand (e.g., 16 → 32).

SVD, Emu-Video, AnimateAnything

Frame Replacement

Replace the first frame’s noise with the clean latent z1. Input becomes {z1, ε2, ..., εn}. Inject first-frame features into spatial attention K,V.

ConsistI2V, TI2V-Zero

Masking Strategy

Conditioning frames are “unmasked” (timestep=0), frames to generate are noised. Different mask patterns enable I2V, continuation, and interpolation from one model.

Open-Sora, Wan 2.1

V2V: Video-to-Video Editing

Takes an existing video and modifies it. Approaches include DDIM inversion + re-denoising with new conditions, instruction-based editing (Movie Gen’s “backtranslation” approach without supervised editing data), and first-frame editing propagated via I2V (AnyV2V). This remains the least mature task.

T2I: Joint Image-Video Training

💡

Key Insight: Images are 1-frame videos

All frontier models treat images as single-frame videos in the same architecture. This is critical because image-text datasets are ~100× larger than video-text datasets, providing far richer visual concepts. Movie Gen uses a 1:3 image:video batch ratio. Open-Sora VAE trains on 20% images + 80% video.

Other Tasks

Camera Control

Plücker coordinates encode each ray as (o × d, d) ∈ R6. A Camera Encoder processes these into features injected via pixel-wise addition into temporal attention layers. CameraCtrl trains on RealEstate10K (~65K clips).

Video Extension & Interpolation

Auto-regressive chunk generation for longer videos. FIFO-Diffusion uses a queue-based mechanism for infinite-length generation. Open-Sora masks first-K-frames for continuation, first+last frames for interpolation.

Subject-Driven Generation

Inject a reference identity via cross-attention adapters (IP-Adapter style). DreamVideo decouples subject learning from motion learning. Movie Gen adds personalization via post-training on human-centric videos with image reference conditioning.

Video Inpainting / Outpainting

Extend or fill missing regions in spacetime. AVID uses Temporal MultiDiffusion for arbitrary-length inpainting. Open-Sora Plan treats I2V itself as “inpainting in the temporal dimension.”

Dense Captions Are Non-Negotiable

Sora's technical report explicitly credits dense captioning as a key factor in generation quality. Short captions (COCO-style, ~10 words) only describe the main action. Dense captions (~100+ words) describe composition, lighting, camera angle, background, style, and motion — giving the model far richer conditioning signal. Models trained on dense captions follow complex prompts dramatically better and produce more diverse, detailed outputs. This is arguably the single highest-leverage data improvement.

💡 The Captioning Recipe

The emerging best practice: (1) Use a large VLM (70B+) on a random subset for high-quality reference captions, (2) distill into a smaller model (7–13B) that can process the full dataset at scale, (3) produce both short (action-centric) and detailed (visual-descriptive) captions for each clip, (4) include structured dimensions like camera motion, lighting, and style. At inference, short user prompts are expanded to match the detailed training distribution (Sora uses GPT to expand prompts, Movie Gen uses LLaMA).

Specific Filtering Techniques

A

Aesthetic Scoring

A CLIP+MLP predictor trained on human aesthetic ratings. LAION-5B aesthetic classifier is widely used. Typical threshold: score >4.5 (retains ~49% of data). Dover model used by HunyuanVideo combines technical + aesthetic quality in a single score.

B

Motion Filtering

Optical flow estimation to compute per-frame motion magnitude. Remove videos that are too static (slideshows, still images with music) or too jittery (handheld shake, fast random cuts). Open-Sora Plan uses 0.001 ≤ motion score ≤ 0.3. VMAF (Video Multi-method Assessment Fusion) scores used by Movie Gen and Open-Sora 2.0.

C

Scene Detection & Splitting

Raw videos are split into single-shot clips using scene boundary detection. PySceneDetect (HunyuanVideo), TransNetV2 neural network (Cosmos), or HSV histogram similarity (Seaweed). This ensures each training clip is temporally coherent with no hard cuts.

D

Deduplication

Semantic deduplication (SemDeDup): compute CLIP embeddings, k-means cluster into 10K+ groups, remove near-duplicates within each cluster (~30% reduction). Movie Gen additionally uses inverse square root resampling by concept cluster to balance the distribution.

Timestep Sampling

How t is sampled during training significantly affects convergence. Uniform sampling wastes compute on trivial noise levels.

Logit-Normal (SD3/FLUX)

Draw t from a logit-normal distribution with parameters (m=0, s=1). This concentrates training on intermediate timesteps where the model refines perceptually meaningful details. Consistently outperforms uniform sampling.

Used by: Movie Gen, HunyuanVideo, Open-Sora, SD3, FLUX

Min-SNR Weighting

Weight the loss by wt = min(SNR(t), γ) with γ=5. Achieves 3.4× faster convergence than unweighted (ICCV 2023). Treats diffusion training as multi-task learning, balancing conflicts among timesteps.

Infrastructure & Parallelism

Video diffusion models are among the most memory-hungry models in AI. A single 720p, 5-second video produces ~73K tokens after patchification — far beyond what fits on one GPU. Distributed training isn't optional, it's a requirement.

Parallelism Strategies for Video DiT Training
1

FSDP (Fully-Sharded Data Parallel)

Shards model parameters, gradients, and optimizer states across GPUs. Each GPU holds only 1/N of the model. All-gather before forward, reduce-scatter after backward. Enables 30B+ params to fit in cluster memory. Used by Movie Gen, Open-Sora 2.0.

2

Sequence / Context Parallelism

The video token sequence (73K+ tokens) is split across GPUs along the sequence dimension. Each GPU processes a chunk, with ring-allreduce for cross-chunk attention. Critical for video: image models don't need this, video models can't work without it. HunyuanVideo uses this as part of its 5D parallelism.

3

Tensor Parallelism

Splits individual weight matrices across GPUs (e.g., each GPU handles a slice of the attention heads). Low communication overhead for intra-node (NVLink). Combined with FSDP for inter-node. Movie Gen uses 3D parallelism: FSDP + tensor + sequence.

4

Pipeline Parallelism

Splits model layers across GPUs (stage 1 on GPU-0, stage 2 on GPU-1, etc.). Micro-batching hides the pipeline bubble. Less common for video DiTs but used in the largest deployments. HunyuanVideo's “5D parallelism” = data + tensor + sequence + context + ZeroCache.

  • Activation Checkpointing: Save only critical activations, recompute during backward pass. ~12% memory savings.
  • Mixed Precision (BF16): BF16 forward/backward, FP32 master weights. Halves activation memory.

GRPO for Video

💡 From LLMs to Video: How GRPO Adapts

You know GRPO from LLMs: generate a group of responses, rank them by reward, update the policy using relative rankings (no critic needed). For video, the same principle applies but with a key challenge: flow matching models use deterministic ODE paths, so there's no natural exploration mechanism.

The solution: convert the ODE to an SDE by adding controlled noise during sampling. This enables the diversity needed for group-relative ranking while keeping the benefits of flow matching.

MixGRPO (Tencent, 2025)

Sliding window approach: SDE sampling only within a window, ODE outside. Reduces training time by ~50% vs DanceGRPO. MixGRPO-Flash reduces by 71%. Used in HunyuanVideo 1.5 production.

This chapter surveys 30+ papers covering every major approach: from training-free noise rescheduling to autoregressive diffusion forcing, hierarchical keyframe generation, and real-time interactive world models.

Why Long Video Is Hard

Most frontier video models generate clips of 4–10 seconds. Extending to minutes or hours introduces three fundamental challenges:

Error Accumulation

Autoregressive generation conditions on previously generated (imperfect) frames. Small errors compound exponentially — characters deform, scenes drift, physics breaks down. This is the exposure bias problem: training on clean data but inferring from self-generated outputs.

Compute & Memory

Self-attention scales quadratically with token count. A 1-minute 720p video has ~100× more tokens than a 5-second clip. Memory requirements grow from 24GB to hundreds of GB, making naive scaling infeasible.

Temporal Coherence

Maintaining consistent character identity, object permanence, scene layout, and narrative logic over minutes requires long-range memory that current architectures lack. Even subtle inconsistencies break immersion.

💡
The train-test discrepancy: During training, models see clean ground-truth frames. During inference, they condition on their own imperfect outputs. Stable Video Infinity (ICLR 2026 Oral) showed this single gap is the root cause of quality degradation in long videos.

Taxonomy of Approaches

Long video methods fall into seven families, each addressing the challenge differently:

Long Video Generation: Method Taxonomy

1. Training-Free Extension Methods

The simplest approach: modify the noise or attention of an already-trained short-video model to generate longer sequences. No retraining required.

FreeNoise (ICLR 2024)

Key idea: Instead of sampling independent noise for each frame, reschedule a fixed sequence of noise frames to achieve both local randomness and long-range temporal correlation.

Two components: (1) Local Noise Shuffling — reuses noise in overlapping windows so nearby frames share structure, and (2) Window-Based Attention Fusion — enables pretrained temporal attention to process any longer sequence by fusing overlapping attention windows.

Only 17% extra time cost vs. 255% for previous methods. Supports multi-prompt generation via motion injection.

Gen-L-Video — Temporal Co-Denoising (2023)

Key idea: Denoise overlapping short clips simultaneously, averaging predictions in overlapping regions to ensure consistency across the full sequence.

Each denoising step runs the base model on sliding windows. Where windows overlap, predicted noise is averaged, creating smooth transitions. No retraining needed — works with any short-video diffusion model.

FreeLong / SpectralBlend Attention (NeurIPS 2024)

Problem discovered: Naively extending short models to longer sequences causes decreased spatial high-frequency components and increased temporal high-frequency components (flickering).

Solution: Decompose attention features into frequency domain. Blend low-frequency components from full-sequence attention (for global coherence) with high-frequency components from adjacent-frame attention (for detail preservation).

🔗
Trade-off: Training-free methods are immediately applicable to any pretrained model, but they cannot fix fundamental architectural limitations. They work best for extending from ~16 to ~128 frames. For minute-scale generation, trained approaches are needed.

2. FIFO & Diagonal Denoising

A family of methods that treat the denoising process as a queue: clean frames exit one end while noise enters the other, enabling theoretically infinite generation.

FIFO-Diffusion: Diagonal Denoising
FIFO-Diffusion (2024)

Key idea: Maintain a queue of frames at progressively increasing noise levels. At each step, jointly denoise all frames in the queue. When the first frame reaches t=0 (clean), dequeue it as output and enqueue a new pure-noise frame at the end.

This diagonal denoising pattern means each frame is denoised in the context of its neighbors, ensuring temporal consistency. The queue size is fixed, so memory and compute are constant regardless of output length — enabling theoretically infinite generation.

Training-free: works with any pretrained video diffusion model. Lookahead denoising with a larger queue window improves quality.

Ouroboros-Diffusion (2025)

Improves on FIFO-Diffusion with Subject-Aware Cross-Frame Attention (SACFA) — detects and tracks subjects across the frame queue, maintaining identity consistency even during camera motion. Also adds structural guidance via self-recurrent denoising: earlier generated frames provide layout constraints for later ones.

3. Autoregressive Diffusion Forcing

The most active research direction (2024–2026). These methods unify autoregressive generation with diffusion by allowing different noise levels per frame.

Diffusion Forcing: Independent Noise Per Token
Diffusion Forcing (NeurIPS 2024, MIT)

Core insight: Train a diffusion model where each token/frame has an independent noise level. This combines the strengths of next-token prediction (variable-length, causal structure) with full-sequence diffusion (global coherence).

At inference, use a sliding window: earlier frames are cleaner (more denoised), later frames are noisier. This creates a natural autoregressive structure without explicit boundary artifacts.

Mathematical Details

Optimizes a variational lower bound on all subsequence likelihoods simultaneously. Given sequence x₁...xₙ, the model is trained with noise levels t₁...tₙ sampled independently per token.

Self Forcing (NeurIPS 2025 Spotlight)

Problem: Diffusion Forcing still trains on clean teacher frames, but at inference conditions on its own (imperfect) outputs.

Solution: During training, simulate inference by performing autoregressive rollout with KV caching. The model trains on its own generated outputs rather than ground truth, directly addressing the exposure bias gap.

Enables real-time streaming video generation on a single RTX 4090. Bridges training and inference distributions.

Rolling Forcing (2025)

Rolling-window joint denoising of multiple frames with progressively increasing noise levels. Unlike strictly causal Self Forcing, frames can mutually refine each other — earlier frames improve later ones, but later ones also refine earlier ones during shared denoising steps.

Achieves multi-minute videos on a single GPU in real-time.

Progressive AR Video Diffusion (PA-VDM, CVPR 2025)

Key idea: Assign per-frame progressively increasing noise levels, then denoise and shift frames in small intervals. Adjacent frames always share similar denoising states, enabling natural temporal coherence.

First to demonstrate text-conditioned 60-second (1440 frame) long video generation at near-frontier quality.

Intuition: Why Progressive Noise Works

Imagine painting a mural: you don't finish each section completely before starting the next. Instead, you sketch the whole outline first (high noise), then progressively add detail from left to right. Each new section is refined in the context of its already-detailed neighbor. This is exactly what progressive AR diffusion does — neighboring frames at similar noise levels naturally attend to each other, maintaining consistency.

4. Hierarchical Generation

Generate sparse keyframes first, then recursively fill in between. Length scales exponentially with recursion depth.

NUWA-XL — Diffusion over Diffusion (ACL 2023)

Architecture: A global diffusion model generates keyframes across the entire time range. Then local diffusion models recursively fill in content between nearby keyframes. This is "Diffusion over Diffusion" — outer diffusion for temporal structure, inner diffusions for frame content.

Demonstrated on 1,024 frames. Parallel inference across hierarchy levels reduces latency by 94% (7.55 min → 26 sec). Length grows exponentially with depth: 2 levels = 256 frames, 3 levels = 1024 frames.

Lumiere (Google, SIGGRAPH Asia 2024)

Takes the opposite approach: generate the entire temporal duration at once through a Space-Time U-Net (STUNet). Processes all frames simultaneously across multiple space-time scales, avoiding the cascading errors of separate keyframe + interpolation pipelines.

Limited to ~5 seconds / 80 frames (by memory), but demonstrates that single-pass full-duration generation avoids temporal artifacts inherent in multi-stage approaches.

LinGen (CVPR 2025)

Replaces quadratic self-attention with a linear-complexity MATE block: Mamba2 branch (bidirectional, long-range) + Temporal Swin Attention branch (local, medium-range). This hierarchical attention enables 68-second video at 16fps on a single GPU — no autoregressive extension needed.

5. Attacking Error Accumulation Directly

Rather than changing the generation paradigm, these methods directly target the error accumulation and position encoding problems.

Stable Video Infinity (ICLR 2026 Oral, EPFL)

Root cause identified: Autoregressive models train on clean frames but inference on self-generated (error-prone) frames. This train-test discrepancy is the fundamental failure mode.

Error-Recycling Fine-Tuning: Collect the DiT's own generation errors, inject them into training data, and teach the model to recognize and correct its own mistakes. Errors are "recycled" through a closed-loop bank.

Achieves infinite-length generation with no additional inference cost. Compatible with diverse conditioning (text, audio, skeleton streams). The error recycling paradigm is orthogonal to other methods — can be combined with any base model.

RIFLEx — Positional Encoding Extension

A remarkably simple fix: reduce the intrinsic frequency of Rotary Position Embeddings (RoPE) to enable 2–3× length extrapolation. This is literally a single line of code change in the model.

Analysis: standard RoPE frequencies alias for positions beyond the training window, causing the model to "hallucinate" that distant frames are nearby. Lowering the frequency eliminates this aliasing.

LoL — Multi-Head RoPE Jitter (2025)

Problem: "Sink collapse." In very long videos with standard RoPE, attention collapses to a few "sink" frames due to RoPE's periodic structure. All generated frames start reverting to these sink frames, causing catastrophic repetition.

Solution: Apply random phase jitter to RoPE frequencies independently per attention head. Different heads see different position encodings, preventing the uniform periodic collapse. Demonstrated on hour-long video with 10,800 frames.

🔢
Noise search optimization: Not all noise tensors are equal. "Bad" initial noise causes errors that compound across chunks. k-Step Noise Search (2024) generates m=10 candidate videos using only k=8 denoising steps each, evaluates via CLIP embeddings, selects the best, then performs full denoising. Cost: 50 extra steps vs. 900+ for brute force.

8. Multi-Scene & Story Consistency

StoryMem (NTU + ByteDance, Dec 2025)

  • Randomized Blending: Enables chunk-wise video enhancement without inter-chunk artifacts

10. Future Directions & Novel Ideas

Memory-Conditioned Generation

The StoryMem/OneStory paradigm — explicit visual memory banks injected via attention — is the most promising near-term approach for narrative coherence. Works with existing pretrained models via LoRA. Key gap: these systems maintain visual consistency but don't reason about plot, causality, or dramatic arc.

3D-Informed Video Generation

GEN3C (NVIDIA, CVPR 2025) uses a "3D cache" — point clouds from depth prediction — to ground generation in explicit geometry. The model conditions on 2D renderings of this 3D cache with user-specified camera trajectories. This elegantly solves consistency by anchoring generation to a geometric representation of the world.

Joint Audio-Video for Long Content

Veo 3's unified latent space for joint audio-video generation is architecturally correct, but scaling to minute-long content with precise temporal alignment (human brain detects >45ms desync) remains an open challenge. LTX-2 and SkyReels-V3 are pursuing open-source alternatives.

Physics-Grounded Generation

PhysVideoGenerator (Jan 2026) injects learned physics priors via cross-attention with physics tokens extracted from V-JEPA 2. Current models achieve <0.60 physics compliance scores across all physical law categories. Explicit physics injection vs. implicit learning from data scale is the key debate.

Real-Time Interactive Worlds

Convergence of Oasis, Genie 3, GameGen-X, and LongLive points toward "video as interactive medium." Remaining barriers: resolution (real-time at 320p–720p), session duration (Genie 3 caps at 60s), and compounding exposure bias in long rollouts.

Narrative-Aware Generation

No current system reasons about plot, character arcs, dramatic tension, or causal story logic. The missing piece is a high-level narrative planner — possibly an LLM — that orchestrates shot composition, pacing, and character development. DreamFactory's multi-agent film crew is an early attempt.

⚠
The fundamental question: Is error accumulation in autoregressive generation irreducible? Solutions like cycle consistency (LIVE) and error recycling (Stable Video Infinity) mitigate but don't eliminate drift. A theoretical breakthrough in maintaining coherence over arbitrarily long sequences may be needed — or the answer may be hierarchical planning + short-horizon generation, avoiding the problem entirely.

Controllable Video Generation

Camera control, motion trajectories, layout guidance, and director-level video creation. From ControlNet adapters to full cinematographic control.

Camera Control Motion Guidance Pose & Layout Director Tools

The Control Challenge

Text-to-video models can generate impressive clips, but text alone is a blunt instrument. Saying "a car drives left" gives no control over how fast, along what path, or from what camera angle. Controllable video generation bridges the gap between vague prompts and precise directorial intent by conditioning on structured signals — camera poses, trajectories, skeletons, bounding boxes, and more.

Precision vs. Generality

Control signals must be specific enough to encode directorial intent yet general enough to work across arbitrary scenes. Plucker coordinates for cameras, sparse point trajectories for motion, and skeleton keypoints for pose each strike different trade-offs.

Disentanglement

Camera motion and object motion are entangled in pixel space. A "zoom in" and an "object approaching" look identical. Methods must decouple global camera transforms from local entity motion to allow independent control.

Multi-Condition Fusion

Real film production requires simultaneous control of camera, character pose, layout, and style. Naively combining conditions causes conflicts — requiring mixture-of-experts routing or hierarchical fusion architectures.

Taxonomy of Control Methods

Controllable video generation methods organize around six primary control modalities, each requiring distinct signal representations and architectural designs:

Controllable Video Generation: Control Modality Taxonomy

1. Camera Control

Camera control methods let users specify exact camera trajectories — pans, dollies, orbits — typically encoded as Plucker coordinates (a 6D per-pixel representation of camera rays) or raw extrinsic matrices (R, T). The core challenge: camera motion is global, affecting every pixel, so it must be injected through temporal attention rather than spatial convolutions.

CameraCtrl (2024) & CameraCtrl II (ICCV 2025)

Key idea: A plug-and-play camera encoder converts Plucker embeddings (n × 6 × h × w) into multi-scale features injected into the temporal attention blocks of a frozen video diffusion model.

The camera encoder uses temporal attention after each convolutional block to capture inter-frame camera relationships. Only the encoder and injection layers are trained; the base model stays frozen. Trained on RealEstate10K (~65K clips).

CameraCtrl II extends to DiT-based models with multi-clip generation: previous clip features stay clean while current clip features are noised, enabling long camera trajectories across clips.

MotionCtrl (SIGGRAPH 2024, Tencent ARC)

Key idea: Separate modules for camera and object motion, trained independently and composed at inference. The Camera Motion Control Module (CMCM) appends camera pose features to temporal transformer self-attention. The Object Motion Control Module (OMCM) injects trajectory features through spatial convolutions.

Design rationale: Camera motion is global → temporal transformers. Object motion is local → spatial convolutions. This decoupling enables independent or combined control.

CamCo (NVIDIA, 2024)

Key idea: Adds Epipolar Constraint Attention (ECA) to enforce 3D geometric consistency. For each pixel in a target view, ECA derives the epipolar line in the source view and restricts cross-attention to features along that line.

Uses Plucker coordinates like CameraCtrl, but the epipolar constraint ensures that generated frames are geometrically plausible — not just visually coherent. Focused on image-to-video with precise camera pose.

VD3D (Snap Research, 2024) & AC3D (CVPR 2025)

VD3D: First work enabling 3D camera control for transformer-based (DiT) video diffusion models. Uses ControlNet-like conditioning with spatiotemporal camera embeddings from Plucker coordinates.

AC3D: Key finding — camera motion is low-frequency, and video DiTs already implicitly estimate camera poses in only a subset of layers. By limiting camera injection to those layers: 4× fewer training parameters, faster convergence, and 10% higher visual quality.

2. Motion & Trajectory Control

Rather than controlling the camera, these methods control what moves and how within the scene. Users provide sparse point trajectories, bounding box sequences, or optical flow fields to direct entity-level motion.

DragNUWA (Microsoft, 2023)

Key idea: Bridge the gap between dense optical flow (available during training) and sparse user trajectories (provided at inference). Three components: a Trajectory Sampler that extracts trajectories from optical flow, Multiscale Fusion that integrates trajectories with text and image at multiple UNet granularities, and Adaptive Training that starts with dense flow then adapts to sparse input.

DragNUWA 1.5 upgraded to Stable Video Diffusion as the backbone for image animation with trajectory control.

DragAnything (ECCV 2024, ShowLab)

Key idea: Entity-level motion control using open-domain latent representations. For each entity, extracts diffusion model latent features based on entity masks, computes a 2D Gaussian around the mask center, and injects entity representation + Gaussian map into the 3D-UNet encoder as multi-scale condition signals.

Unlike DragNUWA, entity representations are open-domain embeddings that work for any object including background. Enables simultaneous distinct motion control for multiple objects. Surpassed DragNUWA by 26% in human voting.

Motion Prompting (CVPR 2025 Oral)

Key idea: Flexible "motion prompts" — from single sparse points to thousands of dense trajectories. The model accepts spatio-temporally sparse or dense point trajectories encoding object-specific or global scene motion, including occlusions and temporally sparse motion.

A motion prompt expansion module translates high-level user requests into semi-dense motion prompts. Camera control achieved via monocular depth estimation + point cloud projection. Unifies camera control, object motion, motion transfer, and image editing in one framework.

Go-with-the-Flow (CVPR 2025 Oral)

Key idea: No architecture changes needed — only modifies data preprocessing. A novel noise warping algorithm replaces random temporal Gaussianity with correlated warped noise derived from optical flow. Preserves spatial Gaussianity while introducing temporal correlation that encodes motion.

Agnostic to diffusion model design. Runs in real-time. Enables local object motion, global camera movement, and motion transfer — all without retraining or architectural modification.

Intuition: Why Noise Warping Works

Standard diffusion starts from random Gaussian noise — each frame's noise is independent. Go-with-the-Flow instead warps the noise using optical flow: if a pixel at (x, y) in frame t moves to (x+dx, y+dy) in frame t+1, the noise value moves with it. The result is noise that already "knows" how things should move. Since the noise remains spatially Gaussian at each frame, the diffusion model can denoise it normally — but the temporal correlations guide the generated motion.

3. Pose-Driven Animation

Given a single reference image of a character and a sequence of target poses, these methods animate the character while preserving identity. The dominant pattern: a ReferenceNet for appearance + a Pose Guider for motion, connected through spatial and temporal attention.

Animate Anyone (CVPR 2024, Alibaba)

Architecture: Three components — (1) ReferenceNet: a symmetrical UNet encoding appearance features, integrated via spatial-attention at each layer; (2) Pose Guider: lightweight module encoding DensePose/skeleton sequences, fused with multi-frame noise; (3) Temporal Layer: temporal-attention for smooth inter-frame transitions.

Two-stage training: First train ReferenceNet + Pose Guider on single frames for image quality, then add the Temporal Layer and train on video clips for coherence.

Block structure: Spatial-Attention → Cross-Attention → Temporal-Attention. Limitations include identity leakage and difficulty with unseen pose distributions.

Champ (ECCV 2024, Fudan University)

Key idea: Go beyond 2D skeletons by leveraging the SMPL 3D body model. Renders four guidance channels from SMPL sequences: depth maps, normal maps, semantic maps, and DWpose skeletons. A guidance encoder with zero convolution extracts and aggregates features from all channels.

A multi-layer motion fusion module uses self-attention to fuse shape and motion in the spatial domain. 3D parametric guidance captures body shape, surface orientation, and part semantics simultaneously.

UniAnimate (Alibaba DAMO, 2024)

Key idea: A unified 3D-UNet that maps reference image, posture guidance, and noise video into a common feature space. Replaces the temporal Transformer with a State Space Model (SSM) for efficient long-sequence handling, reducing quadratic attention to linear complexity.

Supports both random noised input and first-frame conditioned input. Iteratively applies first-frame conditioning to generate >1 minute character animations — significantly longer than Animate Anyone or Champ.

4. Layout & Spatial Control

Layout control specifies where objects should appear using bounding boxes, depth maps, edge maps, or sketches. These methods enable scene composition without pixel-level precision.

GLIGEN (CVPR 2023)

Key idea: Inject spatial grounding into a frozen text-to-image model via new trainable Gated Self-Attention layers at each transformer block. Each layer absorbs bounding box + text label pairs to ground objects spatially. All pretrained weights stay frozen.

Originally image-only, GLIGEN became the foundation for video layout control — extended by LVD, Boximator, and others to handle temporal bounding box sequences.

LVD — LLM-grounded Video Diffusion (ICLR 2024)

Key idea: A fully training-free two-stage pipeline. (1) An LLM generates Dynamic Scene Layouts (DSLs) — bounding box sequences with temporal evolution — from text prompts. (2) A diffusion model generates video from DSLs using classifier guidance.

Both the LLM and diffusion model are used off-the-shelf. Integrates into any video diffusion model that admits classifier guidance. Limitation: the LLM may generate physically implausible layouts.

VideoComposer (NeurIPS 2023, Alibaba)

Key idea: One of the first composable multi-condition video synthesis frameworks. Accepts textual, spatial, and temporal conditions simultaneously: sketch sequences, style images (OpenCLIP ViT-H/14 embeddings), motion vectors (from MPEG-4), depth sequences, and mask sequences.

Two-stage training: (1) Text-to-video for temporal modeling, (2) compositional training with diverse conditions. Users can combine any subset of conditions at inference.

5. Unified Control Frameworks

Rather than building separate models for each control type, these frameworks provide a single adapter architecture that works with any control signal and any base model.

Ctrl-Adapter (ICLR 2025 Oral)

Problem: Pretrained ControlNets cannot be directly integrated into new backbone models due to feature space mismatches. Ctrl-Adapter bridges this gap.

Architecture: Each block contains spatial convolution + temporal convolution + spatial attention + temporal attention. A Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) router assigns per-patch weights for multi-condition fusion — more effective than equal or global weights.

Tested on 6 models (SDXL, PixArt-alpha, I2VGen-XL, SVD, Latte, Hotshot-XL) across both U-Net and DiT architectures. Matches pretrained ControlNets on COCO, achieves SOTA on DAVIS 2017, trains in <10 GPU hours.

ControlNeXt (2024)

Key idea: Replace the heavy parallel branches of ControlNet with a minimal-cost alternative achieving 90% reduction in learnable parameters. The secret: Cross Normalization — normalizes control feature outputs using the mean and variance from the denoising branch, ensuring aligned data distributions.

Cross Normalization replaces Zero-Convolution, enabling convergence in a few hundred steps vs. slow Zero-Conv training. Validated on SD 1.5, SDXL, and Stable Video Diffusion. Seamlessly integrates with LoRA weights for style control without retraining.

💡
Dominant architectural pattern: Nearly all controllable methods follow a "freeze base, train adapter" strategy. The pretrained video model stays frozen while lightweight control modules are injected — as plug-in encoders (CameraCtrl, SparseCtrl), gated attention layers (GLIGEN), epipolar attention (CamCo), or cross-normalization (ControlNeXt). This preserves generation quality while adding precise control.
🔗
Where the field is heading: The trend is toward unified, composable control. Early methods (2023) each handled one signal type. By 2025, frameworks like Ctrl-Adapter and Motion Prompting handle multiple control types simultaneously. Industry systems (Runway Gen-4.5 Director Mode, Kling Motion Brush, Seedance 2.0) now bundle camera, motion, pose, and style control into single products. The remaining frontier is making these controls intuitive — translating high-level directorial language into structured control signals automatically.

Audio-Video Joint Generation

1. The Audio-Visual Challenge

Generating audio that matches video — or generating both simultaneously — requires solving alignment problems that span multiple timescales and abstraction levels.

Temporal Alignment

Sound events must be synchronized to visual events at millisecond precision. A door slam, a footstep, or a lip movement that is off by even 50ms is immediately perceptible. Audio runs at 16–48kHz while video runs at 24–30fps — bridging these vastly different temporal resolutions is non-trivial.

Semantic Correspondence

The generated audio must be semantically correct: a dog should bark (not meow), rain should patter (not thunder in sunshine). This requires the model to understand visual content at a high level and map it to the correct acoustic domain — a cross-modal reasoning problem.

The Modal Gap

Audio and video live in fundamentally different representation spaces. Audio is 1D waveform / spectrogram; video is 3D spatiotemporal pixels. Learning a shared latent space that captures their joint distribution is far harder than learning either modality alone.

💡
Two paradigms: The field splits into video-to-audio (post-hoc synthesis — generate audio conditioned on an existing video) and joint generation (produce both modalities simultaneously from a shared model). The former is more mature; the latter is the frontier.
Audio-Video Generation: Two Paradigms

2. Video-to-Audio Synthesis

Given a silent video, generate a matching audio track. This post-hoc approach is easier because the visual signal provides strong conditioning, but still demands precise temporal sync and semantic accuracy.

Diff-Foley (CVPR 2024)

Key idea: Combine Contrastive Audio-Video Pretraining (CAVP) with latent diffusion to generate high-quality Foley sound from video. CAVP learns a shared audio-visual embedding via contrastive loss; the latent diffusion model then generates mel-spectrograms conditioned on this embedding.

First work to demonstrate high-quality video-to-audio via diffusion. The CAVP alignment module became a foundational component reused by later methods (notably FoleyCrafter).

Two-stage pipeline: CAVP encoder → temporal feature alignment → latent diffusion → vocoder (HiFi-GAN) → waveform.

Movie Gen Audio (Meta, 2024)

Architecture: 13B parameter model built on a T5-based transformer backbone. Takes both video frames and optional text prompts as conditioning. Generates 48kHz high-fidelity audio with synchronized dialogue, ambient sounds, and sound effects.

Joint audio-visual embedding: A pretrained video encoder produces per-frame features that are cross-attended by the audio generation transformer. Text conditioning enables fine-grained control (“footsteps on gravel with distant thunder”).

State-of-the-art quality on multiple benchmarks. Part of Meta’s broader Movie Gen ecosystem for end-to-end video production.

FoleyCrafter (2024)

Key idea: Dual conditioning — semantic and temporal — for video-to-audio. Reuses the CAVP alignment module from Diff-Foley for high-level semantic correspondence, then adds an onset detection mechanism for frame-level temporal synchronization.

The semantic branch ensures the right type of sound is generated; the temporal branch ensures each sound event aligns precisely with its visual trigger. This two-pronged approach substantially improves sync quality over single-conditioning methods.

3. Joint Audio-Video Generation

The harder and more exciting paradigm: generate audio and video simultaneously from a single model, so both modalities are natively coherent rather than stitched together post-hoc.

MM-Diffusion (CVPR 2023)

First joint audio-video diffusion model. Uses a coupled U-Net architecture: separate U-Net branches for audio and video share information via cross-modal attention at each denoising step. Both modalities are denoised together from shared noise.

Demonstrated unconditional joint generation and showed that coupled denoising outperforms independently generating audio and video then combining them. Established the architectural pattern for later joint models.

Veo 3 Google DeepMind, May 2025

First production-scale model with natively synchronized audio-visual generation. Audio and video are denoised together in a unified diffusion process, producing dialogue, ambient sound, and sound effects without any post-processing pipeline. Supports speech generation with natural intonation matched to on-screen characters.

Limited technical details published. Represents a major capability jump but remains a closed system.

LTX-2 Lightricks, Jan 2026

1.5B parameter joint audio-visual foundation model based on a DiT architecture. Supports two modes: (1) joint generation — text prompt produces synchronized video and audio together, and (2) video-to-audio — generates matching audio for an existing video. Lightweight enough for consumer-grade deployment.

Seedance 2.0 ByteDance, Feb 2026

Dual-branch DiT architecture with native audio generation. Separate transformer branches for audio and video tokens exchange information via cross-modal attention layers. Audio — including speech, music, and effects — is generated jointly with the visual stream rather than added as a post-process.

Intuition: Why Joint Generation Beats Cascading

Consider dubbing a foreign film. A human translator first watches the video, then records new audio — but the lip movements never quite match. Now imagine an actor who simultaneously speaks and moves their lips in perfect sync, because both are generated from the same internal representation. Joint audio-video models work like the second case: audio and video tokens attend to each other during generation, so synchronization is structural rather than post-hoc. The shared denoising process means a footstep sound and the foot-hitting-ground frame are literally refined together at every diffusion step.

🔗
The trajectory is clear: The field is moving from two-stage pipelines (generate video, then add audio) toward unified models that produce both modalities from a single forward pass. Joint generation avoids the error propagation and sync drift inherent in cascaded approaches.

4. Text-to-Audio Foundation Models

These standalone audio generation models provide the backbone technology that many video-to-audio systems build upon.

AudioLDM 2 (2024)

Large-scale latent diffusion for audio. Extends the latent diffusion paradigm to audio generation with a “language of audio” representation that unifies speech, sound effects, and music in a single framework. Supports text-to-audio, audio-to-audio, and text+audio-to-audio generation.

Widely used as a backbone for video-to-audio pipelines that condition on visual features rather than (or in addition to) text.

Stable Audio (Stability AI, 2024)

Timing-conditioned latent diffusion. Introduces explicit start-time and duration conditioning, allowing precise control over when audio events occur within a generated clip. Built on a latent diffusion architecture with a VAE operating on mel-spectrograms.

The timing conditioning mechanism is especially relevant for video applications where sound events must occur at specific moments.

MusicGen (Meta, 2024)

Autoregressive music generation. Uses a single-stage transformer language model over multiple parallel streams of compressed audio tokens (from EnCodec). Eliminates the need for cascaded or multi-stage generation. Supports text-conditioned and melody-conditioned music generation.

Demonstrates that autoregressive approaches can rival diffusion for structured audio. The codebook interleaving pattern influences later audio-visual tokenization strategies.

6. Open Challenges

⚠

Remaining Problems in Audio-Video Generation

Lip-sync accuracy: Even the best joint models struggle with precise lip synchronization for speech. Phoneme-level alignment remains an unsolved sub-problem, especially for multilingual content and multiple speakers.

Multi-speaker scenes: Disentangling and spatially localizing audio from multiple simultaneous speakers (the cocktail party problem) is extremely challenging for generative models. Current systems degrade significantly with more than one active speaker.

Foley realism: Generated sound effects often lack the physical plausibility of real recordings — material properties (metal vs. wood), room acoustics, and distance attenuation are poorly modeled. Physically-informed audio generation is an open direction.

Copyright and training data: Audio models are trained on copyrighted music and sound recordings. Legal frameworks are evolving, and the industry lacks consensus on fair use for generative audio, creating deployment risk for commercial applications.

Interactive World Models

Video generation as world simulation: real-time interactive environments, game generation, and the path from video diffusion to general-purpose world models.

Genie 3 Oasis GameGen-X DIAMOND

1. From Video Generation to World Simulation

Standard video generation produces passive clips — the viewer watches but cannot influence what happens. Interactive world models flip this paradigm: the model generates frames conditioned on user actions, creating a real-time feedback loop where each keyboard press, mouse movement, or controller input shapes the next frame.

This vision traces back to Ha & Schmidhuber (2018), who proposed that an agent’s internal "world model" could be a compact neural network that predicts future observations given actions. Their key insight: if the world model is good enough, agents can plan and train entirely inside the model without touching the real environment. The original implementation used a VAE + LSTM — but modern diffusion models provide vastly higher visual fidelity, bringing the vision within practical reach.

Intuition: Video Gen vs. World Model

A video generator is like a movie projector — it plays a predetermined sequence. A world model is like a game engine — it renders the next frame based on what the player does. The fundamental difference is the action-conditioned feedback loop: observe → act → generate next frame → observe → act → ... This loop must run at 20+ FPS to feel interactive, which is 50× faster than typical text-to-video inference.

💡
The paradigm shift: When a video diffusion model can generate consistent, physics-respecting frames in real-time conditioned on arbitrary user input, it effectively becomes a universal simulator — no hand-coded physics engine, no explicit 3D assets, no game logic. Everything emerges from learned weights.

2. Architecture: Action-Conditioned Video Diffusion

The core technical question: how do you inject discrete user actions (keyboard, mouse, controller) into a diffusion model designed for text-conditioned generation?

Three main approaches have emerged: (1) cross-attention injection — encode actions as tokens and attend to them alongside text tokens, (2) latent concatenation — encode actions as spatial feature maps and concatenate with the noisy latent, and (3) latent action models — learn an action space from video without explicit labels (Genie’s approach).

Action-Conditioned Video Diffusion Pipeline
🔗
Latent action models (Genie): Instead of requiring labeled action data, Genie learns a discrete action space from video alone. A VQ-VAE-style encoder maps the transition between consecutive frames to one of N latent action tokens. At inference, user inputs are mapped to these learned tokens. This enables training on unlabeled internet video — a massive data advantage.

3. Key Systems

The field has rapidly progressed from research prototypes to real-time interactive systems in under two years.

Genie 1 (Google DeepMind, Feb 2024)

11B parameters. The first foundation world model, trained on 200K hours of unlabeled internet platformer gameplay video. Three core components:

  • Spatio-Temporal Video Tokenizer (ST-ViViT): Encodes video into discrete tokens capturing both spatial and temporal structure
  • Latent Action Model: Learns a discrete action space (8 actions) from video transitions alone — no action labels needed during training
  • Dynamics Model: MaskGIT-style transformer that predicts next frame tokens given current tokens + latent action

Generated at 1 FPS — far too slow for real-time interaction, but proved the concept of learning controllable world models from passive video.

Genie 2 (Google DeepMind, Dec 2024)

Massive leap in quality. Generates 3D-consistent environments far beyond platformers: photorealistic indoor/outdoor scenes, driving environments, fantasy worlds. Key improvements:

  • 3D consistency: Objects maintain proper perspective and occlusion during camera movement
  • Longer coherent sessions: Extends interactive generation to tens of seconds with consistent world state
  • Diverse environments: Generalizes well beyond training domains to novel scene types from image or text prompts

Used internally as a rapid prototyping environment for training and evaluating AI agents.

Genie 3 (Google DeepMind, Jan 2026)

Public launch. The first generally available interactive world model from DeepMind. Generates diverse environments at 20–24 FPS with significantly improved visual quality and interaction fidelity compared to Genie 2.

Users can create, explore, and remix worlds from text or image prompts. Visual memory extends to approximately one minute. Sessions are currently limited to 60 seconds but demonstrate consistent object permanence and physics throughout.

Represents the Genie lineage reaching real-time interactive quality — a milestone that Genie 1’s 1 FPS made seem years away.

Oasis (Decart AI, 2024)

Real-time Minecraft-like generation at 20 FPS in the browser. No game engine, no hand-coded physics, no 3D assets — everything is pure neural rendering from a DiT backbone paired with a ViT autoencoder.

The model takes keyboard/mouse inputs and previous frames, and generates the next frame in a single forward pass. Achieves 100× faster inference than standard text-to-video models through aggressive architectural optimization and reduced denoising steps.

Demonstrated that interactive world models could run in consumer browsers — a critical milestone for accessibility. Open-source release accelerated community research.

GameGen-X (ICLR 2025)

First DiT for open-world game video generation and interactive control. Two-stage pipeline:

  • Stage 1 — Open-Domain Video Generation: Pretrained on OGameData, a curated dataset of 1M+ gameplay clips from 150+ AAA titles spanning diverse genres and visual styles
  • Stage 2 — Interactive Control via InstructNet: Fine-tuned with instruction-following data to accept character control commands (move, jump, attack) and camera control, producing responsive next-frame predictions

Achieves 20 FPS at 320p with open-domain generalization — meaning it can generate plausible gameplay for game concepts it has never seen.

DIAMOND (NeurIPS 2024 Spotlight)

Diffusion for World Modeling. The first system to train RL agents entirely inside a diffusion-generated world model and achieve superhuman performance. The world model generates Atari game frames conditioned on agent actions, and the agent learns its policy from these generated trajectories.

Key results: mean human-normalized score of 1.46 on the Atari 100k benchmark (26 games) — 46% better than human performance. Also demonstrated on 3D environments like CS:GO, proving the approach extends beyond 2D.

Critical insight: diffusion models provide sharper, more detailed frames than VAE/GAN world models, which translates directly to better RL agent training signal.

LongLive (NVIDIA + MIT, ICLR 2026)

Frame-level autoregressive generation for real-time interactive long video. 1.3B parameters, 20.7 FPS on a single GPU. Two key innovations:

  • KV-Recache: Instead of naive KV-cache that grows linearly with sequence length, periodically re-encodes a compressed context window. This bounds memory while preserving long-range information
  • Frame Sink Token: A special token that accumulates scene-level context across the entire session, preventing "scene forgetting" in long interactions

Users can steer the narrative during generation via streaming text prompts. Demonstrated coherent sessions lasting several minutes.

GameNGen (Google, 2024)

Real-time DOOM generation on a single TPU. Runs the classic first-person shooter at 20 FPS with no traditional game engine — purely neural frame generation. An augmented Stable Diffusion model is conditioned on previous frames and player actions.

Training uses a two-phase approach: (1) an RL agent plays DOOM to generate training trajectories, then (2) the diffusion model learns to predict next frames from action-frame sequences. PSNR of 29.4 dB — comparable to lossy JPEG compression.

Demonstrated that neural game simulation is viable for complex 3D environments with fast-paced action, not just simple 2D games.

4. Reinforcement Learning Inside Generated Worlds

One of the most compelling applications of world models: training RL agents entirely within generated environments. If the world model is accurate enough, agents trained inside it transfer to real environments — dramatically reducing the need for expensive real-world interaction.

DIAMOND demonstrated this is practical. By replacing Atari game emulators with a diffusion world model, agents achieve superhuman scores while only "experiencing" generated frames. The key requirement is that the world model must faithfully reproduce the consequences of actions — incorrect physics or inconsistent reward signals corrupt the learning signal.

5. Open Challenges

Despite rapid progress, interactive world models face fundamental unsolved problems that prevent them from replacing traditional game engines or serving as general-purpose simulators.

3D Consistency

Current models generate plausible-looking frames individually, but struggle with multi-view consistency. Revisiting a location from a different angle often produces a different layout. True 3D understanding — not just 2D image prediction — remains elusive without explicit 3D representations.

Physics Violations

Objects may float, pass through walls, or change mass unpredictably. While the models learn statistical correlations of physics, they lack hard constraints. Gravity, collision, and momentum are approximated, not enforced — leading to subtle but immersion-breaking artifacts.

Memory & Object Permanence

Objects that leave the viewport often vanish or transform when they re-enter. The autoregressive frame-by-frame paradigm has no persistent world state — everything must be reconstructed from the limited context window. Scaling context helps but does not solve the fundamental issue.

Compute Cost

Real-time generation at 20+ FPS requires aggressive optimizations: reduced resolution, minimal denoising steps, and powerful hardware (TPUs/high-end GPUs). Scaling to 1080p at 60 FPS — the standard for modern games — remains orders of magnitude beyond current capability.

⚠️
The fundamental tension: Game engines are exact simulators — every physics calculation, collision check, and state update is deterministic and correct. Neural world models are approximate simulators — they produce statistically plausible outputs but offer no correctness guarantees. Bridging this gap may require hybrid architectures that combine neural rendering with explicit state tracking and physics constraints.

Looking Ahead

The trajectory from Genie 1 (1 FPS, 2D platformers) to Genie 3 (24 FPS, diverse HD worlds) in just two years suggests exponential progress. Key research directions include: persistent world state via external memory, hybrid neural-symbolic physics, multi-agent interactive worlds, and leveraging world models as data engines for robotics and autonomous driving. The ultimate goal — a universal neural simulator that can model any environment at any fidelity — may be the most impactful application of video generation technology.

Efficient Inference & Distillation

Making video generation practical: step distillation, sparse attention, linear attention, caching, quantization, and the path to real-time generation.

Step Distillation Sparse Attention Linear Attention Caching

The Efficiency Crisis

Video diffusion models are 100–1000× more expensive than image diffusion. A single 5-second 720p video clip contains roughly 100K latent tokens. Each of the 50–100 denoising steps requires a full forward pass with self-attention over the entire sequence. The result: minutes to hours of compute on an A100 GPU for a few seconds of video.

Quadratic Attention Cost

Full 3D self-attention over 100K+ tokens scales as O(n²). For a single denoising step this means billions of FLOPs just for attention — and there are 50–100 such steps per video.

Step Redundancy

Standard DDPM sampling requires 50–250 sequential denoising steps. Many of these steps produce nearly identical feature updates, wasting the majority of compute on marginal refinements.

Memory Bandwidth Bottleneck

Large DiT models (5B–14B params) with FP16 weights consume 10–28 GB of VRAM for weights alone. Combined with KV-cache and activations, single-GPU inference is impossible for high-resolution outputs.

⚠

The Latency Gap

Image diffusion models like SDXL generate a 1024×1024 image in ~3 seconds on an A100. A comparable video model generating 5 seconds at 720p takes 2–10 minutes — roughly 100× longer. Bridging this gap requires attacking every layer of the stack simultaneously: fewer steps, cheaper attention, aggressive caching, and lower precision.

Step Reduction & Distillation

The most direct path to speedup: reduce the number of denoising steps from 50–100 down to 1–8, using a student model trained to approximate many teacher steps in a single pass.

Consistency Distillation (Song et al., 2023)

Key idea: Train a student model to map any noisy input directly to the clean output in a single step. The consistency constraint enforces that all points on the same ODE trajectory map to the same final output, enabling 1–4 step generation.

Video application: Latent Consistency Models (LCM) adapt this for video diffusion by distilling the full multi-step ODE into a student that produces coherent temporal outputs in 2–4 steps, achieving 5–10× wall-clock speedup over the teacher.

Trade-off: slight blurriness at 1 step; 2–4 steps recover most detail.

Progressive Distillation (Salimans & Ho, 2022)

Key idea: Halve the number of steps iteratively. In each round the teacher takes 2 DDIM steps and the student learns to match the result in 1 step. Repeat: 128 → 64 → 32 → … → 4 steps.

Each distillation round is relatively cheap because the student is initialized from the teacher. After 5 rounds, a 128-step model becomes a 4-step model with minimal quality degradation.

Used in early Imagen Video and subsequent video pipelines as the baseline distillation strategy.

Rectified Flow (Liu et al., 2023)

Key idea: Straighten the ODE paths connecting noise to data so that fewer Euler steps suffice. Standard flow matching produces curved trajectories that require many steps to trace accurately; rectified flow iteratively reflows to make them near-linear.

InstaFlow demonstrated 1-step image generation by reflowing a pretrained Stable Diffusion model. In the video domain, Wan 2.1 trains with rectified flow from the start, enabling high-quality generation in 20–30 steps instead of 50–100.

Complementary to distillation: straighten first, then distill for even fewer steps.

Adversarial Distillation (ADD / SDXL-Turbo, 2024)

Key idea: Add a GAN discriminator loss to the distillation objective. The student generates in 1–4 steps while a discriminator ensures outputs are perceptually realistic, avoiding the blurriness that plagues pure regression-based distillation.

At 1–4 steps, adversarial distillation consistently produces sharper, more detailed outputs than consistency models. SDXL-Turbo achieved real-time 512×512 image generation; the same principle applies to video with temporal discriminators.

Harder to train due to GAN instability, but currently the best quality at very low step counts.

🔗
Combining strategies: In practice, teams stack these approaches. Rectified flow provides straight ODE paths, consistency or progressive distillation reduces steps to 4–8, and adversarial fine-tuning sharpens the final output. This pipeline can turn a 100-step model into a 4-step model with <5% quality loss.

Attention Efficiency

Even with fewer steps, each step must compute attention over the full spatiotemporal token sequence. For high-resolution, multi-second videos this remains the dominant bottleneck. These methods reduce the per-step cost.

Sliding Tile Attention — STA (ICML 2025)

Key idea: Decompose 3D spatiotemporal attention into tiled windows that slide across the video token grid. Each token attends only to neighbors within its tile, but the sliding pattern ensures every token eventually has an attention path to every other token.

Results: 2.8–17× faster than full attention with no measurable quality loss. Hardware-optimized implementation fuses seamlessly with FlashAttention for maximum GPU utilization.

Drop-in replacement for full attention — no retraining required.

Radial Attention (2025)

Key idea: Exploit perceptual relevance — the center of a video frame carries more information than the periphery. Radial attention defines an O(n log n) sparse pattern where tokens near the spatial center attend densely and peripheral tokens attend sparsely.

Inspired by the human foveal vision system: high acuity at the center, coarser perception at the edges. Particularly effective for videos with centered subjects.

Ring Attention (2024)

Key idea: Distribute the token sequence across multiple devices in a ring topology. Each device computes attention on its local chunk while simultaneously sending/receiving KV blocks from neighbors, overlapping communication with compute.

Enables context lengths of 1M+ tokens — essential for high-resolution, long-duration video. Combined with sequence parallelism, it scales near-linearly with device count.

Used in large-scale training (e.g., Cosmos, Movie Gen) and adaptable to inference.

Linear Attention & State-Space Models

Key idea: Replace quadratic softmax attention with O(n) alternatives. Mamba and S4/S6 state-space models process sequences in linear time by maintaining a fixed-size recurrent state, making them naturally suited for temporal modeling in video.

Some hybrid architectures use full attention for spatial dimensions (where token counts are manageable) and linear attention or SSMs for the temporal dimension (where sequence length grows with video duration).

Emerging approach; not yet dominant but promising for very long video sequences.

Caching & Reuse

Video tokens exhibit massive redundancy — adjacent frames share >90% of visual content, and successive denoising steps produce nearly identical attention patterns. Caching exploits this redundancy.

Token Merging — ToMe

Key idea: During inference, identify and merge similar tokens before each attention layer, reducing the total token count. After attention, merged tokens are unmerged back to their original positions.

Results: Up to 2× speedup with minimal quality degradation. Works as a plug-in module — no retraining needed. Particularly effective for videos with large static regions (e.g., talking heads, landscapes).

Temporal KV-Cache Reuse

Key idea: In autoregressive or sliding-window video generation, reuse computed key-value pairs from previous frames or chunks instead of recomputing from scratch. Only new or changed frames require fresh KV computation.

LongLive uses a KV-recache strategy that enables real-time interactive video generation at 20 FPS by caching and selectively updating KV entries across frames.

Feature Caching — PAB (Pyramid Attention Broadcast)

Key idea: Cache attention outputs from earlier denoising steps and reuse them in later steps, based on the observation that attention maps change slowly across adjacent timesteps.

PAB broadcasts cached attention at different rates for different layer types: spatial attention changes fastest (cache 1–2 steps), temporal attention changes slowly (cache 3–6 steps), and cross-attention is nearly static (cache 6–10 steps). This pyramid strategy delivers 1.5–3× speedup with negligible quality loss.

Quantization & Pruning

Complementary to algorithmic speedups, these hardware-level optimizations reduce the cost of each individual operation.

INT8 / INT4 Quantization

Quantize model weights and activations from FP16 to INT8 (2× memory saving) or INT4 (4×). Post-training quantization (PTQ) with calibration data preserves >95% quality. GPTQ and AWQ are widely adopted for DiT models.

Structured Pruning

Remove entire attention heads or FFN channels that contribute least to output quality. Structured pruning maintains dense matrix operations (unlike unstructured pruning), making it hardware-friendly. Typical savings: 20–40% FLOPs with <2% quality drop.

Mixed-Precision Inference

Use FP16 or BF16 for attention layers (where precision matters) and INT8 for FFN layers (which are more robust to quantization). Combined with FP8 on Hopper GPUs (H100), this delivers near-2× throughput over uniform FP16.

Identity & Character Consistency

Maintaining consistent characters, faces, and objects across video frames and multiple shots. From IP-Adapter to visual memory banks.

Face Preservation Cross-Shot Consistency Subject-Driven Memory Banks

1. The Consistency Problem

Maintaining character identity — face structure, clothing, hairstyle, and object appearance — across frames and shots is one of the hardest unsolved challenges in video generation. Even frontier models routinely produce characters that subtly morph mid-clip or look entirely different after a camera cut.

Intra-Shot Drift

Within a single continuous clip, faces gradually morph: eye color shifts, jawlines reshape, hair texture changes frame-to-frame. This identity drift is caused by the model's denoising process lacking explicit identity anchoring — each frame is generated semi-independently, and small per-frame errors accumulate into visible morphing.

Inter-Shot Inconsistency

When generating multi-shot sequences (e.g., a conversation with alternating camera angles), the same character may appear with a different face, different clothing, or different body proportions across cuts. Without shared identity conditioning, each shot is essentially an independent generation.

Fine-Grained Detail Loss

Even when overall identity is preserved, fine details are lost: accessories disappear, tattoos move or vanish, fabric textures change, logo text becomes unreadable. These high-frequency identity features are the first to be averaged out by the diffusion process.

💡
Why is this so hard? Diffusion models learn to generate plausible images from noise, but they do not inherently maintain a persistent identity state. Each denoising step optimizes for local plausibility, not global identity coherence. The model has no “memory” of who a character is — only what looks reasonable given the current noisy input.

2. Subject-Driven Generation Foundations

Before video-specific solutions emerged, the image generation community developed foundational techniques for conditioning diffusion models on specific subjects. These methods form the backbone of most video identity approaches.

IP-Adapter (Tencent, 2023)

Key idea: Introduce a decoupled cross-attention mechanism for image prompt conditioning. Instead of forcing image and text features through the same attention projection, IP-Adapter adds separate key-value (KV) projection layers for image features alongside the existing text KV projections.

The image features (from a pretrained CLIP image encoder) are injected via their own cross-attention branch, then added to the text cross-attention output. This decoupling allows fine-grained image conditioning without disrupting the text understanding pathway.

Foundation for many video identity methods. The decoupled cross-attention pattern has become the de facto standard for injecting visual identity signals into diffusion models.

DreamBooth (Google, CVPR 2023)

Key idea: Fine-tune the entire diffusion model on just 3–5 images of a specific subject, binding the subject to a rare token identifier (e.g., “[V] dog”). A class-specific prior preservation loss prevents the model from forgetting general concepts while learning the specific subject.

Video extension: DreamBooth-V adapts this approach for video diffusion models, adding temporal consistency losses during fine-tuning to ensure the learned subject identity remains stable across generated frames.

High fidelity but requires per-subject fine-tuning (3–5 minutes per subject on a single GPU). Not suitable for real-time or zero-shot applications.

Textual Inversion (2023)

Key idea: Learn a new embedding vector in the text encoder's embedding space that represents a specific subject. Only the embedding is optimized — the rest of the model stays frozen. The learned embedding can then be composed with natural language prompts.

Extremely lightweight (a single vector per subject), but limited expressiveness. Complex subjects with fine-grained details are poorly captured by a single text-space embedding. Often used in combination with other methods.

3. Face & Character Preservation in Video

Building on the image-domain foundations, these methods tackle the specific challenge of preserving face and character identity in generated video sequences.

PhotoMaker (2024)

Key idea: Create a stacked ID embedding from multiple input photos of the same person. Face features from several reference images are extracted, stacked, and merged into the text embedding space via a learned projection. This fused identity embedding replaces the class token in the text prompt.

Advantages: (1) multiple photos capture different angles and expressions, producing a more robust identity representation; (2) no per-subject fine-tuning at inference — the projection is trained once and generalizes to unseen identities; (3) fast inference, comparable to standard text-to-image generation.

InstantID (2024)

Key idea: Combine a semantic face embedding (from ArcFace/InsightFace) with spatial face keypoints via two parallel conditioning pathways: (1) IdentityNet — a lightweight adapter that injects the face embedding into cross-attention layers for global identity; (2) Face Adapter — a ControlNet-style module that uses facial landmark keypoints to guide spatial structure (pose, expression).

This dual-signal approach decouples who the person is (semantic embedding) from how they are positioned (spatial keypoints), enabling zero-shot identity-preserving generation with controllable pose and expression.

ConsistI2V (2024)

Key idea: A spatiotemporal conditional diffusion framework for image-to-video generation that preserves the identity and layout of the first frame throughout the generated clip. Two key components:

  • Layout-preserving first-frame conditioning: The reference image is injected not just at the initial timestep but throughout the denoising process via spatial cross-attention, anchoring identity across all frames.
  • FrameInit noise initialization: Instead of pure random noise, the initial noise tensor is constructed to encode low-frequency layout information from the first frame, giving the denoising process a head start on structural consistency.
StoryDiffusion — Consistent Self-Attention (NeurIPS 2024 Spotlight)

Key idea: A training-free mechanism that shares self-attention features across frames/images to maintain character consistency in multi-shot storytelling. During the self-attention computation, tokens from reference frames are incorporated into the attention of all generated frames, building cross-image identity connections within a single batch.

For video generation: a Semantic Motion Predictor estimates inter-frame motion conditions in semantic space, converting the consistent image sequence into a temporally smooth video while preserving the shared identity features.

ID-Animator (2024)

Key idea: Identity-preserving video generation using a dedicated face adapter module trained on a large-scale face video dataset. The adapter encodes a reference face image into identity tokens that are injected into the video diffusion model’s cross-attention layers at every temporal frame.

By training on real face videos (rather than static images), the adapter learns to produce identity embeddings that are naturally robust to the temporal variations (motion blur, expression changes, lighting shifts) encountered during video generation.

💡
Face encoders matter: Most identity-preserving methods rely on pretrained face recognition models (ArcFace, InsightFace, FaceNet) as their identity signal source. The quality of the face encoder directly bounds the quality of identity preservation — if the encoder cannot distinguish two similar faces, neither can the generation model.

4. Multi-Shot & Story Consistency

Single-clip identity preservation is necessary but not sufficient. Generating coherent multi-scene narratives requires maintaining character consistency across independent shots — a fundamentally harder problem because each shot may be generated separately with no shared latent state.

StoryMem (NTU + ByteDance, Dec 2025)

Key idea: A visual memory bank that stores and retrieves character embeddings across scenes. After each shot is generated, key identity features (face embeddings, appearance descriptors) are extracted and stored in an external memory module. When generating subsequent shots, the model queries this memory bank to retrieve relevant identity features, injecting them via latent concatenation with negative RoPE shifts and LoRA adaptation.

28.7% improvement in cross-shot consistency over base models. The external memory approach decouples identity storage from the generation model, enabling scalable multi-character tracking.

DreamFactory (2025)

Key idea: An end-to-end framework for multi-scene story video generation with character consistency. Takes a script or storyboard as input and generates a coherent multi-shot video where characters maintain their appearance across all scenes. Integrates identity conditioning, scene layout planning, and temporal transition modeling into a unified pipeline.

Targets the full story-to-video workflow rather than individual shot generation, addressing the gap between single-clip identity methods and practical filmmaking needs.

Runway Gen-4 (2025)

Key idea: Reference image conditioning for cross-shot consistency in a commercial video generation system. Users provide one or more reference images of characters, and the model conditions all generated shots on these references to maintain visual consistency across an entire project.

Represents the productization of identity consistency research. While architectural details are not fully published, it demonstrates that reference-conditioned cross-shot consistency is reaching commercial viability.

Video Editing

AI-powered video editing modifies existing footage while preserving unedited regions, motion, and temporal structure. This chapter surveys 15+ methods spanning training-free attention manipulation, instruction-based editing, first-frame propagation, and video inpainting.

Editing vs. Generation

Video editing is fundamentally different from video generation. Generation creates footage from scratch (text, image, or noise). Editing takes an existing video and modifies it — changing appearance, adding or removing objects, or altering style — while preserving everything that should remain untouched. This constraint introduces three core challenges:

Temporal Preservation

Edited frames must maintain the original video's motion, timing, and temporal dynamics. Even small per-frame inconsistencies create visible flickering. The edit must blend seamlessly with the source video's temporal structure.

Spatial Selectivity

Edits should affect only the targeted region or attribute. Unedited areas — backgrounds, untouched objects, lighting — must remain pixel-accurate to the original. This requires precise spatial masks or attention control.

Faithful Inversion

Most diffusion-based editing methods require inverting the source video into the model's latent space (typically via DDIM inversion). Errors in this inversion accumulate across frames, causing drift and artifacts in the edited output.

Taxonomy of Approaches

Video editing methods fall into four major families, each trading off flexibility, quality, and compute:

Video Editing: Method Taxonomy

1. Training-Free (Zero-Shot) Editing

These methods manipulate the attention maps or internal features of pretrained diffusion models at inference time. No training or fine-tuning is required, making them immediately applicable to any base model.

FateZero (ICCV 2023 Oral)

Key idea: Fuse attention maps from the source video's DDIM inversion pass into the editing (denoising) pass. During inversion, the model stores cross-attention and self-attention maps at every timestep. During editing, these stored maps are blended with the new target-prompt attention maps using a spatial mask derived from the source word's cross-attention.

Attention blending: Afused = mask × Asource + (1 - mask) × Atarget, where the mask is thresholded from the cross-attention of the word being edited. This preserves unedited regions while allowing targeted changes.

Fully zero-shot. First to demonstrate zero-shot video style editing. Supports style, attribute, and shape-aware edits via spatial-temporal self-attention.

TokenFlow (ICLR 2024)

Key idea: Consistency in the edited video is enforced by maintaining consistency in the diffusion feature space. Inter-frame correspondences are computed from the source video's diffusion features, then used to propagate edited tokens from keyframes to all other frames.

Token propagation: Tiedit = ∑j wij × Tjkeyframe-edit, where wij are nearest-neighbor correspondence weights from original features. Keyframes are jointly edited using extended-attention blocks, then their tokens propagate to non-keyframes via the pre-computed correspondences.

Training-free, works with any T2I editing method. User study: 94% preference over PnP-Diffusion, 78% over Text2Video-Zero.

RAVE (CVPR 2024)

Key idea: Grid-based video editing. Rearranges video frames into a spatial grid (e.g., 2×2 or 3×3), applies image editing to the entire grid as a single image, then reassembles frames. By placing multiple frames in one image, the diffusion model's spatial attention implicitly enforces cross-frame consistency.

Also uses noise shuffling — randomly shuffling noise latents between frames at each denoising step — to force spatio-temporal coherence without explicit temporal attention modules.

Remarkably simple yet effective. Processes videos in a fraction of the time and memory of TokenFlow or FateZero. Handles longer videos than explicit attention methods.

COVE (NeurIPS 2024)

Key idea: Consistent Video Editing via diffusion feature correspondence. Computes token-level similarity across frames in the source video's diffusion features, then uses a sliding-window correspondence mechanism to guide self-attention during the editing pass.

During denoising, self-attention is restricted to tokens with high inter-frame correspondence — ensuring that corresponding regions across frames receive consistent edits. Temporal token merging reduces GPU memory overhead.

Seamlessly integrates into pretrained T2I models. No training or optimization needed. Handles both global (style) and local (object) editing.

🔗
Trade-off: Training-free methods are immediately applicable to any pretrained model and require zero additional data. However, they struggle with large structural changes (adding/removing objects) because they rely on the source video's spatial structure via inversion. For more dramatic edits, instruction-based or first-frame methods are preferred.

2. Instruction-Based Editing

These methods train models to follow natural language editing instructions, analogous to InstructPix2Pix for images but extended to video with temporal consistency mechanisms.

InstructVid2Vid (ICME 2024)

Key idea: Extends InstructPix2Pix to video by modifying Stable Diffusion with a conditional 3D U-Net that generates temporally coherent frame sequences. Adds an explicit inter-frame consistency loss during training that penalizes perceptual differences between adjacent edited frames.

Training data is synthesized by combining LLMs, image editors, and video generators to create video-instruction-edited triplets — more cost-efficient than collecting real paired data.

Supports appearance changes, style modifications, and object attribute editing. Temporal consistency degrades on longer videos.

InsV2V (ECCV 2024)

Key idea: Inflates InstructPix2Pix with temporal attention layers and trains on 400K+ synthetic video editing pairs. Uses DDIM inversion of the source video to initialize the editing pass, combined with temporal attention across frames for coherence.

Employs dual classifier-free guidance: one scale (sI) controls fidelity to the input video, another (sT) controls adherence to the text instruction. This allows users to balance preservation vs. edit strength.

EVE — Emu Video Edit (ECCV 2024 Oral, Meta)

Key idea: Factorized diffusion distillation. Trains an image editing adapter and a video generation adapter separately, both attached to the same T2I backbone. The image adapter teaches precise per-frame editing; the video adapter ensures temporal consistency. Joint unsupervised distillation aligns both — no supervised video editing data required.

State-of-the-art on the TGVE benchmark. Supports adding, removing, and changing texture of objects, plus style transfer and background replacement.

Movie Gen Edit (Meta, 2024)

Key idea: Part of Meta's Movie Gen system. A 30B parameter transformer trained end-to-end for precise video editing with natural language instructions. Accepts a source video plus a text instruction, and targets only the relevant pixels while preserving everything else.

Supports both localized edits (add, remove, replace specific elements) and global edits (background change, style modification). The massive scale and paired training data give it robustness that zero-shot methods cannot match.

3. First-Frame Propagation

A rising paradigm: edit just the first frame using any image editing tool, then propagate the edit to the full video using an image-to-video (I2V) model or optical flow. This decouples the editing quality (handled by mature image editors) from temporal propagation (handled by I2V models).

AnyV2V (TMLR 2024)

Key idea: Edit the first frame with any off-the-shelf image editor (InstructPix2Pix, ControlNet, IP-Adapter, DragEdit, etc.), then use a pretrained I2V model to propagate the edit across all frames. The source video is DDIM-inverted, and during sampling, spatial convolution features, spatial attention features, and temporal attention features from the source are injected into the edited generation.

Modularity: The image editor and I2V model are completely interchangeable. As image editors and I2V models improve, AnyV2V automatically benefits without any retraining.

Supports prompt-based, reference-based, subject-driven, and local region editing — all by swapping the first-frame editor.

FlowVid (CVPR 2024, Meta)

Key idea: Optical flow-guided propagation of an edited first frame. Rather than strictly following flow (which contains errors), FlowVid warps the edited first frame to subsequent frames using optical flow and feeds these warped frames as supplementary conditioning to the diffusion model.

The model jointly uses spatial conditions (depth maps) plus the flow-warped reference to generate temporally consistent output, gracefully handling flow imperfections.

3.1× faster than CoDeF, 7.2× faster than Rerender-A-Video, 10.5× faster than TokenFlow. User preference: FlowVid 45.7% vs. TokenFlow 40.4%.

💡
Why first-frame propagation is winning: Image editing is a more mature field than video editing — tools like InstructPix2Pix, ControlNet, and IP-Adapter are already excellent. Meanwhile, I2V models (CogVideoX, Wan 2.1, Kling) are improving rapidly. First-frame methods like AnyV2V combine the best of both worlds: state-of-the-art image editing quality with state-of-the-art video generation consistency. As either component improves, the full pipeline automatically benefits.

4. Video Inpainting

A specialized form of video editing focused on filling in masked regions — removing unwanted objects, completing occluded areas, or adding new content within a defined spatial mask while maintaining temporal coherence.

ProPainter (ICCV 2023)

Key idea: Dual-domain propagation combined with an efficient mask-guided sparse transformer. Propagates information in both the image domain (global correspondence from distant frames via flow warping) and the feature domain (local correspondence from nearby frames). A sparse transformer then refines only the tokens within and near mask regions, dramatically reducing compute.

Pipeline: Content encoding → recurrent flow completion → dual-domain propagation → mask-guided sparse transformer → frame decoding.

+1.46 dB PSNR over prior methods. 0.12 seconds per frame. Applications: object removal, video completion, watermark removal.

VideoPainter (SIGGRAPH 2025)

Key idea: A plug-and-play video inpainting framework compatible with any video DiT architecture. Supports any-length video inpainting by operating in a streaming fashion, overcoming the fixed-length limitation of prior methods. Integrates seamlessly as an add-on module without requiring architectural changes to the base video generation model.

1. From 2D Video to 3D Worlds

Video generation models implicitly learn rich 3D structure — objects have consistent geometry across frames, cameras move through coherent scenes, and lighting obeys physical rules. The central question of this chapter: can we extract or enforce explicit 3D from these learned priors?

Three paradigms have emerged, each trading off fidelity, speed, and controllability:

Distillation

Use a pretrained 2D/video diffusion model as a loss function to optimize a 3D representation (NeRF, Gaussians, mesh). The diffusion model acts as a “critic” that evaluates renderings from every angle. Pioneered by DreamFusion (SDS) and extended to 4D by MAV3D.

Feed-Forward

Directly generate multi-view images or videos from input, then reconstruct 3D in a single forward pass. Faster than distillation but requires multi-view training data. Examples: SV3D generates orbital videos; CAT3D produces multi-view sets from any number of input images.

Hybrid

Combine explicit 3D geometry (point clouds, depth maps) with video diffusion for geometrically grounded generation. The 3D cache provides structural scaffolding while diffusion fills in photorealistic detail. GEN3C and ViewCrafter are leading examples.

2. Score Distillation Sampling (SDS)

The foundation technique that enabled text-to-3D and text-to-4D generation. SDS turns any pretrained diffusion model into a loss function for optimizing 3D parameters.

DreamFusion — SDS (Poole et al., 2022)

Core idea: Optimize a NeRF so that its renderings “look like” samples from a pretrained image diffusion model. At each iteration: render the NeRF from a random camera, add noise, ask the diffusion model to denoise, and backpropagate the difference as a gradient signal.

Intuition: The Art Critic Analogy

Imagine asking an art critic (the pretrained diffusion model) to evaluate your 3D sculpture from every possible angle. The critic doesn’t sculpt — they only say “this rendering doesn’t look realistic enough, here’s what should change.” You iteratively adjust the sculpture based on this feedback from thousands of viewpoints. SDS formalizes this loop: the diffusion model provides the gradient direction, and the 3D representation follows it.

VSD — Variational Score Distillation (ProlificDreamer, NeurIPS 2023)

Key insight: SDS treats 3D parameters as a point estimate, leading to mode-seeking behavior. VSD instead models them as a distribution, replacing the random noise baseline ε with a learned noise predictor εψ (implemented as a LoRA-finetuned copy of the diffusion model).

This creates a two-player game: the pretrained diffusion model provides the target distribution, while the LoRA model tracks the current distribution of renders. The result is photorealistic, diverse outputs without the oversaturation artifacts of SDS.

Supports standard CFG (7.5), produces 512×512 photorealistic renders — a major quality leap over SDS.

3. 3D-Consistent Video Generation

These methods combine explicit 3D representations with video diffusion models to achieve geometrically grounded, camera-controllable video generation.

GEN3C (NVIDIA, CVPR 2025 Highlight)

Architecture: Built on NVIDIA Cosmos (7B parameters) with a CNN + Transformer hybrid. The key innovation is a 3D point cloud cache — pixel-wise depth prediction unprojected into 3D — that provides explicit geometric conditioning for video generation.

How it works: When generating new frames, the model renders the 3D cache from the target camera trajectory and uses these renderings as conditioning. Unlike prior models that must “remember” geometry or infer it from camera poses alone, GEN3C has direct access to explicit 3D structure.

Autoregressive refinement: Newly generated frames update the 3D cache, enabling temporally consistent long-form video with precise camera control. Achieves state-of-the-art on sparse-view novel view synthesis, including challenging driving scenes and monocular dynamic video.

Applications: world simulation, autonomous driving, AR/VR content creation.

SV3D (Stability AI, ECCV 2024)

Core approach: Adapts Stable Video Diffusion (SVD) for multi-view synthesis from a single image. Two variants: SV3Du (unconditional orbital video) and SV3Dp (camera-path-conditioned generation via Plücker ray coordinates).

Key insight: The temporal coherence built into SVD naturally enforces view consistency across generated frames — a significant advantage over image-based multi-view generators like Stable Zero123. The video architecture’s temporal attention acts as an implicit multi-view consistency mechanism.

Fine-tuned from SVD on Objaverse 3D renders. Major improvements in generalization and view consistency over prior methods.

ViewCrafter (TPAMI 2025)

Core approach: Bridges explicit 3D geometry with generative video models. A coarse point cloud (from depth estimation) provides geometric guidance, while video diffusion fills in photorealistic detail and handles occlusion.

Iterative expansion: Starting from initial views, ViewCrafter progressively extends 3D coverage through camera trajectory planning. Each iteration generates new views, which are added to the point cloud. The final accumulated representation is optimized into a 3D Gaussian Splatting model for real-time rendering.

Applications: immersive VR walkthroughs, scene-level text-to-3D, creative content generation.

🔗
The hybrid pattern: Both GEN3C and ViewCrafter follow the same recipe — use off-the-shelf depth models to build coarse 3D geometry, then condition video diffusion on renderings of that geometry. This “3D cache + diffusion” paradigm is emerging as the dominant approach for camera-controllable generation with geometric grounding.

4. Text-to-4D & Dynamic 3D

Generating dynamic 3D content — objects and scenes that move over time and can be viewed from any angle. These methods extend score distillation to the temporal domain.

MAV3D / Make-A-Video3D (Meta, ICLR 2024)

First text-to-4D method. Optimizes a dynamic NeRF (HexPlane representation) using SDS from Meta’s Make-A-Video text-to-video model. HexPlane renders image sequences from random cameras, scored by the T2V model against the text prompt.

Three-stage optimization: (1) Static 3D scene from image SDS, (2) add temporal dynamics via video SDS, (3) super-resolution refinement. Requires no 3D or 4D training data — the T2V model’s prior is sufficient for 4D optimization.

Limitations: long optimization time (hours), limited motion range, output quality bounded by the video model.

Animate124 (2024)

Single image to 4D. First work to animate an in-the-wild image into 3D video through textual motion descriptions. Input: one image + text describing desired motion.

Three-stage pipeline: (1) Static NeRF initialization from the reference image using 2D + 3D diffusion priors, (2) motion learning via video diffusion guidance on a textured mesh representation, (3) refinement with a personalized diffusion prior to combat semantic drift during optimization.

SV4D (Stability AI, ECCV 2024)

Extends SV3D to 4D. A unified latent video diffusion model that generates multi-frame and multi-view consistent novel view videos from a single image + monocular reference video.

Key innovation — frame-anchor attention: A novel multi-view temporal consistency mechanism where the first frame of each view serves as an anchor in self-attention computation. This ensures spatial consistency across views while preserving temporal coherence within each view. The generated multi-view videos directly supervise dynamic NeRF optimization without cumbersome SDS.

Performance: 5-frame videos across 8 views in ~40 seconds; full 4D optimization in 20–25 minutes.

4DGen (2024)

Grounded 4D from single-view video. Generates dynamic 3D content from monocular video by combining multi-view synthesis with dynamic 3D Gaussian Splatting.

Pipeline: (1) Multi-view diffusion generates consistent novel views for each video frame, (2) anchor frames initialize a 4D Gaussian representation, (3) temporal propagation fills remaining frames via deformation fields with spatial-temporal regularization. No SDS optimization required — uses direct multi-view supervision for faster convergence and higher detail preservation.

Intuition: From Distillation to Feed-Forward

Early text-to-4D methods (MAV3D, 4D-fy) optimize a single scene for hours using SDS — like sculpting by committee feedback. Newer methods (SV4D, 4DGen) generate multi-view videos directly and reconstruct 3D in minutes — like having a photographer capture the scene from every angle simultaneously. The field is steadily shifting from slow per-scene optimization toward fast feed-forward generation.

2. Audio-Driven Methods

Audio-driven methods take a single reference image and an audio clip as input, generating a video of the person speaking with synchronized lip movements, natural expressions, and head motion.

SadTalker (CVPR 2023)

Key idea: Use a 3D Morphable Model (3DMM) as an intermediate representation. Two specialized networks predict motion coefficients from audio: ExpNet learns expression coefficients (lip shape, eyebrow movement) via distilled coefficients from a pretrained model, and PoseVAE generates diverse but realistic head poses using a conditional VAE.

The predicted 3DMM coefficients are rendered into an unsupervised 3D keypoint volume, which drives a face vid2vid renderer to produce the final video. Decoupling expression from pose avoids the rigid, unnatural results of earlier methods.

First to achieve realistic and diverse head motion from audio alone without explicit landmark supervision.

AniPortrait (2024)

Key idea: A clean two-stage pipeline — Audio2Mesh converts audio to a sequence of 3D facial mesh vertices, then Mesh2Video uses a mesh-conditioned video diffusion model (built on a reference-guided U-Net) to synthesize the final video.

The mesh intermediate provides explicit geometric control, allowing the model to handle both audio-driven and video-driven portrait animation through the same second stage. Supports face reenactment by swapping the mesh source.

Hallo — Hierarchical Audio-Driven Visual Synthesis (2024)

Key idea: Build audio-conditioned portrait animation on the AnimateDiff framework. Audio features are injected through a hierarchical set of adapter modules targeting three motion scales: a lip adapter for precise phoneme-to-viseme mapping, an expression adapter for mid-frequency emotional changes, and a pose adapter for low-frequency head movement.

Training strategy: Progressive training — first train on images with the reference net frozen, then add temporal layers with video data, and finally fine-tune all adapters jointly. This prevents catastrophic forgetting and enables high-fidelity generation.

Hallo2 (2024)

Key idea: Extends Hallo to 4K resolution and long-duration generation (minutes of continuous video). Key additions: (1) adjustable semantic text embedding for pose style control (head stability vs. expressiveness), (2) temporal alignment and augmentation techniques for long-sequence consistency, and (3) a vector quantization-based face enhancement module for 4K upsampling.

First audio-driven portrait system to produce minutes-long 4K output with controllable head stability.

EchoMimic (2024)

Key idea: Dual conditioning with audio features and facial landmarks for portrait animation. Uses parallel reference paths: one encodes identity from the reference image, while audio and landmark encoders separately inject motion signals. Supports both audio-only and audio+landmark modes.

A landmark-guided denoising process produces spatially precise lip sync, while audio conditioning ensures natural rhythm. The model can be driven by audio alone (landmarks auto-generated) or by user-supplied landmarks for fine-grained control.

MuseTalk (2024)

Key idea: Real-time lip sync by operating entirely in latent space. Instead of generating full frames, MuseTalk modifies the lower-face region in VAE latent space conditioned on audio features. A lightweight audio encoder maps mel-spectrogram windows to latent modifications, enabling inference at 30+ FPS on a single GPU.

Optimized for production use cases (video conferencing, dubbing, virtual presenters) where speed matters more than full-head animation fidelity.

🔗
Evolution of audio-driven methods: The field progressed from 3DMM-based explicit representations (SadTalker) through mesh intermediates (AniPortrait) to end-to-end diffusion approaches (Hallo, EchoMimic). Each generation trades explicit geometric control for greater expressiveness and quality, while MuseTalk shows that latent-space shortcuts can achieve real-time performance for targeted applications.

3. Video-Driven & Reenactment

Video-driven methods transfer facial motion from a driving video to a source identity, enabling cross-identity reenactment without audio.

LivePortrait (2024)

Key idea: Efficient portrait animation via implicit keypoint-based motion representation with learned stitching and retargeting modules. Instead of explicit 3DMMs or landmarks, LivePortrait learns compact keypoint representations that naturally decompose into identity-agnostic motion.

Three lightweight modules enable practical deployment: (1) a stitching module that blends the animated face seamlessly into the original frame, (2) eye retargeting for gaze control, and (3) lip retargeting for fine-grained mouth shape transfer. Achieves real-time inference (~12 ms per frame on GPU) making it one of the fastest portrait animation methods.

Open-source and widely adopted for its speed-quality tradeoff.

MegActor (2024)

Key idea: Combines unconditional generation with conditional motion driving to produce megapixel-resolution portrait animations. The model first learns a strong face generation prior, then conditions on driving signals for motion transfer. This decoupled design improves identity preservation and handles challenging cross-identity scenarios.

X-Portrait (2024)

Key idea: Cross-identity expressive portrait animation using diffusion models. X-Portrait focuses on transferring fine-grained expressions (micro-expressions, asymmetric movements) across very different face shapes and identities. Uses a dedicated expression disentanglement module to separate identity-specific appearance from transferable expression features.

4. Full-Body & Digital Humans

Moving beyond face-only animation, these methods generate complete talking humans with co-speech gestures and body movement.

TANGO (2024)

Key idea: Full-body talking avatar with co-speech gesture synthesis from audio alone. TANGO jointly models lip sync, facial expressions, hand gestures, and upper-body motion conditioned on speech audio. The gesture generation module uses a diffusion-based motion prior trained on large-scale speech-gesture datasets to produce semantically meaningful gestures synchronized with speech rhythm and content.

Outputs full-body skeleton sequences that drive a neural renderer, enabling gesture styles ranging from casual conversation to formal presentation.

CyberHost (ByteDance, 2025)

Key idea: Production-grade audio-driven digital human system producing high-quality full-body animation with natural gestures and precise lip sync. CyberHost integrates a body motion generation network conditioned on audio prosody with a high-fidelity neural rendering pipeline, targeting real-time streaming applications such as virtual hosts, live commerce presenters, and digital customer service agents.

Represents the industry frontier for full-body digital human generation, achieving broadcast quality at interactive frame rates.

6. Open Challenges

⚠️
Persistent challenges in talking head generation:
  • Teeth and tongue artifacts: The oral cavity is one of the hardest regions to synthesize. Most methods produce blurry, distorted, or missing teeth, and tongue movement during speech is rarely modeled correctly.
  • Extreme head rotations: Large pose changes (>45° from frontal) cause severe quality degradation, as the model must hallucinate unseen regions of the face and hair.
  • Temporal stability over long sequences: Identity drift, flickering textures, and inconsistent lighting accumulate over multi-minute generation, especially for autoregressive methods.
  • Real-time performance: Most diffusion-based methods require seconds per frame. Achieving real-time (>30 FPS) generation at high resolution remains an engineering challenge, with only latent-space methods (MuseTalk) and keypoint-based methods (LivePortrait) reaching interactive speeds.
  • Ethical concerns: Talking head technology is dual-use. The same methods that enable virtual presenters and accessible communication also power deepfakes. Watermarking, detection methods, and responsible deployment policies remain critical open problems for the field.

Physics & World Simulation

Making generated videos physically plausible: physics priors, world consistency evaluation, and the debate over whether video models are world simulators.

Physics Priors World Consistency Evaluation 3D Grounding

1. Do Video Models Understand Physics?

When OpenAI launched Sora in February 2024, they described it as a “world simulator” — a model that learns to represent physical reality through large-scale video prediction. The claim was provocative: if a model can generate realistic videos of complex scenes, has it learned physics?

Proponents argued that training on millions of hours of real-world video naturally encodes intuitive physics — gravity, collisions, material properties — because these regularities pervade the training data. Sora demonstrated impressive emergent behaviors: camera reflections, 3D consistency through motion, and plausible fluid dynamics in certain scenes.

Critics quickly pushed back. Within weeks, researchers catalogued systematic failures: objects phasing through each other, gravity reversing mid-scene, liquids defying fluid dynamics, and chairs spontaneously gaining or losing legs. These weren’t rare edge cases — they appeared in the majority of generated clips once you looked beyond cherry-picked demos. The model had learned what physics looks like in still frames, but not how physics works across time.

The truth likely lies somewhere in between, and measuring exactly where has become a research field in itself. The following sections survey the failure modes, evaluation benchmarks, and proposed solutions that define the physics-in-video-generation landscape as of early 2026.

💡
The central question: Do video generation models learn a compressed representation of physical laws from data, or do they learn a high-dimensional texture-matching function that only appears physically plausible in short clips? The answer has profound implications: if the former, scaling alone might yield true world simulators. If the latter, explicit physics integration will be necessary.

2. What Models Get Wrong

Systematic evaluations reveal three consistent failure modes across all current video generation models, from open-source to frontier. These failures are not model-specific — they appear in Sora, Kling, Runway Gen-3, CogVideoX, and every other system tested, varying in severity but never absent:

Object Permanence

Objects spontaneously appear, disappear, duplicate, or change identity when temporarily occluded. A ball rolling behind a pillar may emerge as a different color, shape, or not at all. Characters walking behind obstacles frequently change clothing, hairstyle, or body proportions. Models lack a persistent object representation — they re-hallucinate objects from context each time they re-enter the frame.

Gravity & Collisions

Dropped objects float, fall at incorrect rates, or accelerate sideways. When objects collide, they frequently interpenetrate rather than bounce or deform. Stacked objects hover without support. Thrown objects follow parabolic arcs that flatten or curve incorrectly. These failures reveal that models learn a weak prior for “things fall down” but not the dynamics of gravitational acceleration (9.8 m/s²) or conservation of momentum.

State Changes

Eating food doesn’t reduce the amount on the plate. Pouring liquid doesn’t lower the source level or raise the destination level. Candles burn without melting. Ice doesn’t melt. Breaking objects produces fragments that don’t conserve mass. Models treat each frame as a semi-independent generation conditioned on recent context, failing to track cumulative physical state changes over time.

💡
Why these failures matter: Physics errors break immersion far more than visual artifacts. Viewers unconsciously track physical plausibility — a slightly blurry background is forgettable, but a cup that floats in mid-air is immediately jarring. As video generation targets professional content creation, physics correctness becomes the critical quality bar.

3. Physics Evaluation Benchmarks

A growing family of benchmarks attempts to move beyond subjective “looks real” evaluations toward systematic, quantitative measurement of physical understanding. These benchmarks are critical for tracking progress and identifying specific failure modes.

PhysBench / T2VPhysBench (2025)

Scope: Systematic evaluation of physics understanding across five domains: mechanics (gravity, collisions, friction, elasticity), fluid dynamics (pouring, splashing, viscosity), thermodynamics (melting, boiling, heat transfer), optics (reflections, refractions, shadows), and material properties (rigidity, deformability, breakage).

Each domain contains 50–200 carefully designed test prompts with paired evaluation criteria. Results are sobering: most frontier models score 30–60% on basic physics questions that humans answer at 95%+. Performance varies dramatically by domain — optics and shadows are relatively well-handled (60%+), while fluid dynamics and state changes remain below 35%.

The benchmark reveals an important pattern: models perform best on physics that manifests as static visual cues (shadows, reflections) and worst on physics that requires tracking state evolution over time (fluid flow, melting).

PhysGen (2024)

Key idea: Evaluate physical plausibility by comparing generated videos against physics simulations. Given a scene description, PhysGen runs both a video generator and a physics engine (MuJoCo / PyBullet), then measures trajectory divergence, penetration depth, energy conservation, and momentum transfer accuracy.

Advantage: provides physically grounded, continuous-valued metrics rather than binary “plausible / not plausible” judgments. Limitation: restricted to scenes that can be set up in standard physics simulators.

VideoPhy (ICLR 2025)

Multi-category physics benchmark spanning rigid body dynamics, soft body deformation, fluid interactions, and multi-object systems. Defines two evaluation protocols:

  • Text-to-Video: Given a text description of a physical scenario, generate a video and evaluate against physics ground truth.
  • Video Prediction: Given initial frames of a physical process, predict the continuation and compare against real-world recordings.

The video prediction protocol is more diagnostic — it isolates physics understanding from visual generation quality by providing the initial conditions directly. Results show that even when given correct starting frames, models struggle to predict physically correct continuations.

WorldScore (2025)

World consistency score evaluating generated videos on three orthogonal axes:

  • 3D Consistency — do objects maintain correct geometry and scale under viewpoint changes?
  • Temporal Persistence — do objects and scene elements persist correctly across frames?
  • Physical Plausibility — do dynamics obey approximate physical laws?

WorldScore aggregates these into a single composite metric, enabling model comparison on “world simulation quality” rather than just perceptual fidelity. Frontier models score 0.55–0.72 (out of 1.0), with 3D consistency being the weakest axis.

🔗
Benchmark limitations: All current physics benchmarks have significant blind spots. They test a limited range of physical phenomena (mostly rigid body mechanics), use automated evaluation that may miss subtle errors, and struggle to evaluate long-horizon physical reasoning. A video where an ice cube slowly melts over 30 seconds requires tracking gradual state change — most benchmarks evaluate only short clips (2–5 seconds). Building comprehensive physics evaluation remains an open challenge.

4. Approaches to Physics Grounding

Researchers are pursuing five complementary strategies to close the physics gap. These range from data-centric approaches (changing what the model sees during training) to architecture-level interventions (changing how the model processes physical information):

Strategy 1: Physics-Informed Training Data

Strategy: Curate training data from physics simulation engines such as Kubric (Google), ThreeDWorld (MIT/IBM), and Isaac Sim (NVIDIA). These engines produce photorealistic video with perfect physics annotations: ground-truth depth, surface normals, object trajectories, contact forces, and material properties.

Mixed training on simulation + real video improves physical understanding without sacrificing visual realism. The key challenge is the sim-to-real gap: models must learn to transfer physics knowledge from synthetic domains to the full diversity of real-world scenes. Domain randomization (varying textures, lighting, and object properties in simulation) helps bridge this gap but does not fully close it.

Strategy 2: Physics-Conditioned Generation

Strategy: Condition the video generation model on outputs from a physics engine — depth maps, surface normals, force vectors, or coarse simulation trajectories — as additional input channels alongside text prompts.

This decouples what happens (physics engine) from what it looks like (video model). The physics engine handles dynamics; the video model handles appearance. Trade-off: requires running a physics engine at inference time, which limits generality to scenes that can be simulated.

Strategy 3: PhysDreamer (2024)

Key innovation: Given a static video of an object, PhysDreamer estimates the object’s physical material properties (Young’s modulus, Poisson’s ratio, density) from visual appearance using a learned prior. It then feeds these estimated properties into a differentiable physics simulator (Material Point Method) to generate physically plausible object dynamics — how the object would deform, bounce, or break under applied forces.

This approach marries learned video priors with explicit physics: the video model provides material understanding, while the physics engine guarantees dynamical correctness. Results show realistic soft-body deformation, cloth draping, and elastic bouncing that pure video models cannot achieve.

PhysDreamer represents a compelling middle ground: using neural networks for what they do well (material recognition from appearance) and physics engines for what they do well (dynamical simulation from known properties).

Strategy 4: Explicit Simulation Integration

Strategy: Run physics engines alongside the generation model during both training and inference. Differentiable rendering pipelines (e.g., NVDiffRast, 3D Gaussian Splatting) enable backpropagation through physics → render → loss, training the model to produce outputs consistent with simulated physics.

The key enabler is differentiable physics: engines like Warp (NVIDIA), DiffTaichi, and Brax support gradient computation through the simulation step, allowing end-to-end training of generation + physics pipelines.

Most promising for domain-specific applications (robotics, engineering visualization) where the scene can be fully specified. Less applicable to open-domain creative generation where scene setup is ambiguous.

Strategy 5: Reward Models for Physics Correctness

Strategy: Train a reward model to score the physical plausibility of generated videos, then use reinforcement learning (RLHF/RLAIF) during post-training to optimize for physics correctness. The reward model can be trained on human judgments of physical plausibility, outputs from physics simulators, or both.

This approach integrates naturally with the RLHF pipelines already used for aesthetic quality and safety. Key challenge: physics reward must be granular enough to guide specific improvements (e.g., “the ball should bounce higher”) rather than just binary plausibility judgments. Early experiments show that even coarse physics rewards (trained on PhysBench-style data) improve benchmark scores by 8–15 percentage points during post-training.

🔗
Trade-off summary: Data-centric approaches (simulation training data, reward models) are the most scalable and compatible with existing training pipelines. Architecture-level approaches (physics conditioning, explicit simulation) offer stronger guarantees but are harder to generalize. PhysDreamer occupies a compelling middle ground. In practice, frontier labs are pursuing all five strategies simultaneously.

5. The World Simulator Hypothesis

The debate over whether video models are “world simulators” touches on fundamental questions about what neural networks learn from data. Two views have crystallized:

The emergentist view holds that sufficient scale and data will naturally produce physics understanding. Just as LLMs develop reasoning capabilities that weren’t explicitly programmed, video models may develop physical intuition as an emergent property of predicting the visual world. Evidence in favor: models trained on more data and at larger scale consistently show better physics, and some emergent capabilities (like 3D consistency) appear suddenly at certain scale thresholds.

The structuralist view argues that statistical pattern matching, no matter how sophisticated, cannot replace explicit physical reasoning. A model that learns “balls usually go down” from millions of videos has a useful heuristic, but it cannot reason about why or predict what happens in novel configurations. Evidence in favor: physics benchmarks show sublinear improvement with scale, and even the largest models fail on simple scenarios absent from training data.

A practical resolution may be emerging: video models are useful world simulators even if they are not correct ones. For applications like robot pretraining, creative content, and game development, approximate physics that covers 90% of common scenarios may be sufficient — the remaining edge cases can be handled by explicit physics or human oversight. The question then becomes not “do they understand physics?” but “are they reliable enough for the task at hand?”

💡 Intuition: Statistical Physics vs. Real Physics

Video models learn statistical correlations that approximate physics: balls tend to go down, water tends to flow, objects tend not to interpenetrate. But they don’t learn the underlying equations. They are more like a very good artist who knows what physics looks like but can’t solve F=ma.

Consider: a model trained on millions of videos of balls dropping will learn that balls accelerate downward. But it won’t learn why (gravitational force), so it can’t generalize to novel situations — a ball on the Moon, a ball in a centrifuge, two balls of different mass. A physics engine handles all these cases from a single equation; a video model needs examples of each.

This suggests a hybrid future: video models for appearance and common-case physics, explicit simulation for correctness and generalization.

6. Open Questions: Closing the Physics Gap

Physics understanding remains the widest gap between generated and real video. While visual quality has approached photorealism in many domains, physics correctness lags by 30–40 percentage points on standardized benchmarks. Closing this gap is not just an academic exercise — it determines whether video generation models can be trusted for applications in robotics simulation, autonomous driving, scientific visualization, and professional filmmaking.

💡

Can We Close the Physics Gap?

Three complementary paths are emerging:

  • More and better training data: Physics simulation engines can generate unlimited physically-correct video. As sim-to-real transfer improves, models trained on mixed real + synthetic data may internalize physical laws more deeply. The bottleneck is photorealism and domain diversity of simulation engines.
  • Explicit physics integration: Hybrid architectures that route physics-critical computations through differentiable simulators while using neural networks for appearance. PhysDreamer and physics-conditioned generation point the way, but general-purpose integration remains unsolved.
  • Physics-aware reward models: RLHF-style post-training with rewards specifically targeting physical correctness. This requires building reliable automated physics evaluators — essentially solving the evaluation problem (benchmarks above) to create training signal. The most scalable path if reward models can be made accurate enough.

The likely outcome is all three in combination: simulation-enriched pretraining data, physics-conditioned architectures for critical applications, and physics reward models for general-purpose quality improvement. The physics gap is arguably the single most important frontier in video generation — solving it would move these models from impressive visual toys to genuine tools for understanding and simulating the physical world.

One additional question looms: is perfect physics even necessary? For creative applications like filmmaking and games, physically plausible video (looks right to a human viewer) may suffice — exact Newtonian correctness is less important than visual coherence. For scientific and engineering applications (robotics sim, crash testing, fluid design), physical accuracy is essential.

The field may bifurcate: approximate physics from reward-tuned models for creative use, and hybrid neural-simulation pipelines for precision applications. Both paths are being actively explored, and the next generation of video models will likely offer tunable physics fidelity as a key differentiator.

Open Problems & Research Frontiers

Despite rapid progress, video generation faces fundamental challenges. These represent the most important research directions for the next 1-3 years.

Current Limitations

⚠ Physics Simulation

Models don't accurately model physical interactions: glass shattering, fluid dynamics, object permanence. Eating food doesn't change object state. Objects float, interpenetrate, or behave non-physically.

T2VPhysBench (2025), World Consistency Score

⚠ Long-Form Consistency

Each generation is typically 4–8 seconds. Longer videos cause drift in character appearance, scene layout, and style. Autoregressive extension introduces compounding errors.

⚠ Fine Detail Control

Complex facial expressions, fine hand movements, and small text remain challenging. Compositional prompts with multiple entities and relationships often fail.

⚠ Audio-Video Sync

Separately generated audio and video inevitably desynchronize. Veo 3 addresses this with joint generation, but it remains unsolved for most models.

⚠ Real-Time Generation

Even optimized inference can't produce high-resolution video in real-time. A 5s 720p video takes hundreds of seconds to generate.

⚠ Reward Hacking

RL alignment reward models can be exploited by the optimizer. Videos may score high on automated metrics while looking worse to humans. Multi-dimensional, robust reward models are needed.

Research Frontiers

Unified RL Frameworks

DanceGRPO and MixGRPO demonstrate single algorithms working across diffusion and flow matching, across T2I/T2V/I2V. Expect further unification and 10× efficiency gains.

Physics-Grounded Generation

World Consistency Score and T2VPhysBench represent early steps. Future models may incorporate learned physics priors or physical simulation engines as reward signals.

Constitutional Video Generation

No equivalent to Constitutional AI exists for video. "Temporal risk" — where individual frames are harmless but sequences are unsafe — is a unique video challenge requiring novel safety approaches.

World Models

As video models evolve toward world simulators (Sora's vision), they must learn physical consistency, causal reasoning, and factual accuracy — not just visual plausibility.

Efficient Alignment

MixGRPO-Flash (71% training time reduction) and ReinFlow (single-step fine-tuning) show RL alignment needn't be prohibitively expensive. Making alignment practical at video scale is an active frontier.

Multi-Objective Pareto Alignment

Optimizing quality vs. safety vs. text faithfulness vs. motion simultaneously rather than a single scalar reward. MJ-VIDEO's MoE and VisionReward's MPO are early approaches.
