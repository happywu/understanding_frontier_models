Unified Understanding & Generation: The Omni Model Revolution

A comprehensive guide to omni models — systems that can both comprehend and create across text, images, video, and audio within a single architecture. Covers Transfusion, decoupled encoders, discrete tokenization, and the frontier models reshaping AI in 2024–2026.

Understanding Generation Unified Models 2024–2026 Frontier

What Are Omni Models?

An omni model is a single neural network that can both understand existing content (answer questions about images, reason about video, follow text instructions) and generate new content (produce images, audio, or video from prompts) — all within one unified architecture. This is a fundamental departure from the previous paradigm where understanding and generation required entirely separate systems.

📍

Why Unification Matters

When understanding and generation share the same model, the system can leverage its world knowledge while creating. GPT-4o can generate historically accurate scenes because the same transformer that learned history also produces the pixels. A pipeline of separate models loses information at every handoff. Unified models also enable conversational creativity — iteratively refining images through natural dialogue, maintaining context across turns.

The Historical Separation

For years, AI maintained a clean division between two families of models, each optimized for fundamentally different objectives:

Understanding Models

Models like BERT, GPT-4V, LLaVA, and BLIP-2 excel at reasoning, question answering, captioning, and classification. They use semantic visual encoders (CLIP, SigLIP) that produce high-level abstract features aligned with language.

Encoders: CLIP / SigLIP | Objective: Next-token prediction (cross-entropy) | Attention: Causal
Generation Models

Models like DALL-E, Stable Diffusion, Midjourney, and Imagen create images and video from text prompts. They use diffusion or GAN architectures that work with fine-grained pixel-level detail in continuous latent spaces.

Encoders: VAE / VQ-VAE | Objective: Denoising (MSE / diffusion loss) | Attention: Bidirectional

These two families evolved independently because their requirements are in direct tension: understanding needs abstract, high-level semantic features while generation needs precise, low-level spatial details. Attempting to serve both from a single visual encoder forces suboptimal trade-offs in both directions.

The Convergence Moment (2024–2025)

Several breakthroughs converged in 2024–2025 to make unification both feasible and inevitable:

Transfusion (Meta, August 2024)

Proved that a single transformer can be trained with both next-token prediction (for text) and diffusion loss (for images) without degradation in either modality. The two losses are simply added together. This was the key technical unlock — directly enabling GPT-4o’s native image generation.

Result: 34× more compute-efficient than Chameleon for image generation quality
Janus / Janus-Pro (DeepSeek, Oct 2024 – Jan 2025)

Showed that the understanding-generation conflict can be resolved by decoupling visual encoders (separate SigLIP for understanding, VQ tokenizer for generation) while sharing a single transformer backbone. Simple, effective, and the best-performing approach at small scale.

Janus-Pro-7B: GenEval 0.80 (outperforms DALL-E 3, SDXL, SD3)
Chameleon & Emu3 (Meta / BAAI, 2024)

Demonstrated the pure next-token prediction philosophy: convert everything — text, images, video — into discrete tokens, train a single transformer with a single cross-entropy loss. Chameleon pioneered training stability techniques (QK-Norm, z-loss). Emu3 was published in Nature (2025).

Chameleon: 9.2T tokens, 34B params | Emu3: 32,768 codebook, ~8.5B params
GPT-4o & Gemini (OpenAI / Google, 2025)

Frontier commercial models shipped native image generation directly inside the LLM, replacing external DALL-E / Imagen pipelines. GPT-4o uses a Transfusion-style approach; Gemini uses sparse MoE with native multimodal training. Both support conversational editing and knowledge-grounded generation.

GPT-4o: #1 on Artificial Analysis Image Arena | Gemini 2.5: 1M token context

The Fundamental Unification Problem

Why is it so hard to build a single model that excels at both understanding and generating visual content? The answer lies in a deep conflict between the representations each task requires.

The Representation Conflict

Understanding and generation require fundamentally different kinds of visual features. A model that understands images needs to know what is in the scene; a model that generates images needs to know exactly how each pixel looks. These two goals pull the visual encoder in opposite directions:

RequirementUnderstandingGeneration
Visual representation High-level semantic features Fine-grained pixel-level details
Information granularity Abstract, conceptual Precise, spatial
Optimal encoder CLIP / SigLIP (text-aligned) VQ-VAE / VAE (reconstruction-focused)
Attention pattern Causal (sequential reasoning) Bidirectional (spatial coherence)
Training objective Next-token prediction (cross-entropy) Reconstruction / denoising (MSE / diffusion)

A single visual encoder optimized for understanding tends to lose the fine-grained spatial information needed for high-quality generation. Conversely, a pixel-level encoder retains spatial detail but lacks the semantic abstraction needed for reasoning. This is not merely a training challenge — it is a structural tension in what information the representation must preserve.

The Seesaw Effect

When researchers attempt to train a unified model with a single shared encoder, they consistently observe a seesaw effect: improving generation performance degrades understanding, and vice versa. This is not a training bug — it is a direct consequence of the representation conflict.

Janus provides the most rigorous experimental evidence for this effect. The team tested four encoder configurations while keeping everything else constant:

Only by decoupling (Exp-D) can both tasks reach their potential simultaneously. This experiment is the single strongest piece of evidence that the representation conflict is real and architectural, not merely a training issue.

💡 Analogy: The Architect vs. The Painter

Imagine asking one person to be both an architect and a photorealist painter. The architect needs to understand buildings at a structural level — load-bearing walls, room flow, spatial relationships — ignoring surface details. The painter needs to reproduce every crack in the brick, every shadow, every texture. Training as an architect builds habits of abstraction that fight against the painter’s attention to pixel-level detail. A single visual encoder forced to serve both tasks is like asking this person to think abstractly and concretely at the same time, using the same mental representation. The result is compromised performance on both tasks.

The solutions in the field correspond to different resolutions of this analogy: decoupled encoders (Janus) hire two specialists who share an office; Transfusion gives the painter different tools (continuous patches + diffusion) while the architect keeps blueprints (discrete tokens + cross-entropy); all-discrete approaches (Chameleon, Emu3) try to find a universal visual language good enough for both.

Three Core Challenges

Challenge 1: Encoder Conflict

Janus’s ablation study provides the clearest evidence. Using VQ tokens alone for understanding gives only 60.1 POPE. Using SigLIP alone gives 82.4. Mixing both in a shared encoder achieves ~75 — worse than decoupling (87.0). A single encoder cannot be simultaneously optimized for semantic abstraction and pixel-level reconstruction without sacrificing one or both.

Challenge 2: Training Instability

Mixed-modal training at scale is notoriously unstable. Chameleon discovered that different modalities have vastly different entropy distributions, causing softmax logit norms to grow uncontrollably. Without QK-Norm, training diverges after ~20% of an epoch. Image generation training is the primary source of instability — a model trained without it does not diverge.

Challenge 3: Quantization Bottleneck

Discrete-token approaches (VQ-VAE) lose information during quantization. Chameleon acknowledges a “core weakness” in reconstructing images with text, upper-bounding OCR-related capabilities. Codebook size limits expressiveness: 8,192 codes (Chameleon) vs. 32,768 (Emu3) vs. continuous patches (Transfusion, which avoids the bottleneck entirely). Image quality is bounded by the tokenizer.

Why Early Attempts Failed

The idea of unified understanding and generation is not new. Several earlier systems attempted it but fell short:

CM3Leon (Meta, 2023)

Token-based autoregressive model for interleaved text and image generation. Demonstrated the concept but image quality was far below diffusion models. The VQ tokenizer was too lossy, and the model lacked stability techniques for mixed-modal training at scale.

GILL & DreamLLM (2023)

Early attempts to add generation capability to LLMs by training adaptor layers to an external diffusion model. Generated images lacked fidelity, and the pipeline approach (LLM → adaptor → diffusion model) lost semantic information at each interface boundary. Not truly unified.

Flamingo / BLIP-2 (2022–2023)

Powerful understanding models that bolted vision encoders onto frozen LLMs. Could comprehend images brilliantly but could not generate a single pixel. The frozen-encoder “late fusion” approach made generation architecturally impossible.

What Changed: Two Key Breakthroughs

Visual Tokenization Matured

The quality of VQ-VAE and VAE tokenizers improved dramatically in 2023–2024. Emu3’s MoVQGAN uses a 32,768-code codebook (4× larger than Chameleon’s). MAGVIT-v2 introduced lookup-free quantization. Most importantly, continuous tokenization (Transfusion, NextStep-1) eliminated the quantization bottleneck entirely by keeping images as continuous latent vectors rather than forcing them into a discrete codebook.

Mixed-Objective Training Became Feasible

Transfusion proved that cross-entropy + diffusion loss can coexist in a single transformer without mutual interference. The key innovations: bidirectional attention within images (3× FID improvement), lightweight U-Net encoder/decoder (only 3.8% parameter overhead at 7B scale), and proper loss balancing (λ=5 for image loss). The result was 34× more compute-efficient than Chameleon for equivalent image quality.

💡

Chameleon’s Stability Discovery

Chameleon’s most important contribution may not be the model itself but the stability techniques it developed. The team found that output norm growth is “strongly correlated with predicting future loss divergence” — making it an early warning signal. They also discovered that image generation training is the sole source of instability: a model trained without image generation does not diverge, confirming the mixed-entropy hypothesis. Later systems (Show-o on Phi-1.5, Janus on DeepSeek-LLM) sidestepped this by building on pre-trained LLMs rather than training from scratch.

⚠

The Tension Is Not Fully Resolved

Even the best current systems make trade-offs. Emu3 requires separate post-training variants (Emu3-Gen for generation, Emu3-Chat for understanding) despite having a unified pre-trained backbone. Janus-Pro’s decoupled encoders add parameters. Transfusion’s dual-loss training is more complex to implement and tune. The field has found workable solutions, not a perfect one. The open question remains: can a single visual encoder ever be truly optimal for both tasks?

📍

Where the Field Stands

The representation conflict is real and well-documented, but it is no longer a blocker. Three architectural strategies — decoupled encoders, hybrid AR+diffusion, and high-quality discrete tokenization — each provide viable paths to unification. The question has shifted from “is unification possible?” to “which approach scales best?” The evidence so far: Transfusion-style hybrids dominate at the frontier (GPT-4o, NextStep-1), while decoupled encoders (Janus-Pro, BAGEL) offer the best balance at smaller scale.

Visual Tokenization

The critical enabling technology: converting images and video into token sequences that language models can process. From VQ-VAE to lookup-free quantization and beyond.

VQ-VAE / VQGAN MAGVIT-2 TiTok Discrete vs Continuous

Why Tokenization Is the Bottleneck

Language models operate on discrete tokens. Images exist as continuous pixel arrays. The visual tokenizer is the bridge — and its quality determines the upper bound on what any unified model can achieve. A tokenizer with poor reconstruction quality will produce blurry, incoherent images no matter how powerful the language model is. MAGVIT-2 proved this decisively: with a good enough tokenizer, vanilla LLMs surpass diffusion models at image generation.

💡

The Core Insight

Tokenizer quality is THE determining factor in whether autoregressive LLMs can compete with diffusion for visual generation. Codebook size, quantization strategy, and reconstruction fidelity matter more than the generator architecture itself.

A visual tokenizer has three components: an encoder that maps pixels to latent representations, a quantizer that discretizes (or preserves) those latents, and a decoder that reconstructs pixels from the representation. The key metrics are rFID (reconstruction quality, lower is better), codebook utilization (percentage of codes actually used), and the number of tokens per image (fewer is better for LLM efficiency).

VQ-VAE & VQGAN: The Foundations

VQ-VAE (2017) — Neural Discrete Representation Learning

The first clean formulation of discrete visual tokenization. A CNN encoder maps images to continuous latent vectors, which are then mapped to the nearest entry in a learned codebook of K embedding vectors. The decoder reconstructs the image from these quantized latents. A straight-through estimator passes gradients through the non-differentiable argmin operation during backpropagation.

Codebook: 512 entries | Architecture: CNN encoder/decoder | Limitation: blurry reconstructions (no perceptual/adversarial loss)
VQGAN (2021) — Taming Transformers for High-Resolution Image Synthesis

VQGAN = VQ-VAE + perceptual loss (LPIPS) + adversarial loss (PatchGAN discriminator). The perceptual loss ensures semantic fidelity while the GAN loss ensures sharpness, enabling strong compression with high visual quality. This became the de facto standard for discrete image tokenization through 2023.

Codebook: 1K–16K entries | Tokens/image: 256 (f=16) or 1,024 (f=8) | rFID: ~1.3 (ViT-VQGAN) to ~7.9 (original) | Used by: Chameleon, DALL-E, Parti
VQ-VAE / VQGAN Tokenization Pipeline

VQ-VAE Training Loss

The VQ-VAE loss has three terms: reconstruction, codebook alignment, and a commitment term that prevents the encoder outputs from drifting too far from codebook entries:

  • Term 1 (reconstruction): Mean squared error between input and decoded output
  • Term 2 (codebook loss): Moves codebook entries toward encoder outputs (sg = stop-gradient on encoder)
  • Term 3 (commitment loss): Prevents encoder outputs from growing unbounded; β typically 0.25

MAGVIT-2: Lookup-Free Quantization

Google's MAGVIT-2 (ICLR 2024) proved the central thesis of visual tokenization: with a good enough tokenizer, vanilla LLMs surpass diffusion models. The key innovation is Lookup-Free Quantization (LFQ), which replaces the entire codebook-lookup mechanism with a simpler binary quantization scheme.

MAGVIT-2 — Lookup-Free Quantization (LFQ)

Instead of maintaining an explicit codebook and computing nearest-neighbor distances, LFQ projects the encoder output to D dimensions and binarizes each dimension independently to {-1, +1} via the sign function. The resulting D-bit binary vector IS the code — no lookup needed. The effective codebook size grows exponentially: D=18 gives 218 = 262,144 codes. An entropy penalty ensures full utilization of all possible codes, eliminating codebook collapse entirely.

Codebook: 218 = 262,144 (implicit) | Tokens/image: 256 (16×16) | rFID: ~1.17 | Compression: surpasses HEVC codec

Intuition: From dictionary lookup to binary addresses

Traditional VQ is like looking up a word in a physical dictionary — you search through K entries to find the closest match. LFQ is like using a binary address system: each dimension votes yes or no, and the combination of votes uniquely identifies one of 2D possible entries. No searching required, no stored dictionary needed, and every address is reachable by construction.

MAGVIT-2 also introduced token factorization for efficient prediction: instead of predicting one token from a vocabulary of 262,144, the model predicts two sub-tokens, each from a vocabulary of 512 (factorizing 18-bit codes into two 9-bit codes). This dramatically reduces the softmax computation cost.

TiTok: 1D Tokenization

TiTok (NeurIPS 2024) broke a fundamental assumption: image tokens do not need to live on a 2D spatial grid. Using a Vision Transformer with learnable query tokens that attend to image patches via cross-attention, TiTok produces a 1D sequence of compact tokens — as few as 32 tokens for a 256×256 image.

TiTok NeurIPS 2024

An image worth 32 tokens for reconstruction and generation. 1D tokens are ordered but not spatially constrained — they capture information in a compressed, abstract manner. At 512×512 resolution, TiTok achieves 410× faster generation than DiT-XL/2 while producing better images (gFID 2.74 vs 3.04). A two-stage training process first trains the full encoder-quantizer-decoder, then fine-tunes the decoder for improved reconstruction.

💡

Why 1D Matters for Omni Models

1D token sequences align perfectly with text token sequences in LLMs. Reducing image representation from 256–1,024 tokens (2D grid) to 32–64 tokens (1D) provides a massive speedup for autoregressive generation, where cost scales quadratically with sequence length. This makes practical interleaved text-image generation feasible.

Cosmos Tokenizer: Dual-Mode Architecture

NVIDIA's Cosmos Tokenizer (2024) provides both continuous AND discrete tokenizers for images and video within a unified architecture, using a novel wavelet-based front-end for initial compression.

Cosmos Tokenizer — Continuous & Discrete Modes

The architecture uses a Haar wavelet transform for lossless 4× spatial+temporal downsampling (no learnable parameters), followed by causal temporal convolution and attention layers. For discrete mode, it uses Finite Scalar Quantization (FSQ) instead of VQ. For continuous mode, it outputs unquantized latents directly. The causal design enables frame-by-frame streaming video processing.

Variants: 10 (CI/DI/CV/DV at multiple compressions) | Max compression: 2,048× (DV8x16x16) | 8× more compression than prior SOTA | Up to 12× faster

BSQ: Binary Spherical Quantization

BSQ (ICLR 2025) combines the efficiency of binary quantization with geometric structure by projecting latent vectors to a hypersphere before binarizing. This geometric constraint provides better gradient flow, more uniform codebook utilization, and superior scaling to large codebook sizes compared to LFQ.

BSQ-ViT ICLR 2025

Achieves an rFID of 0.41 on ImageNet 256×256 — 43% better than the runner-up. Uses a ViT encoder/decoder with configurable binary dimensions (L=12 to 36), giving effective codebook sizes from 4,096 to 68 billion. The codebook is entirely implicit: zero learnable codebook parameters. Block-wise causal masking enables unified image+video tokenization, and throughput is 2.4× faster than prior methods.

The Design Space

Every omni model must answer three fundamental questions: How do you represent visual information? (discrete tokens vs. continuous latents) How do you generate images? (autoregressive vs. diffusion vs. hybrid) Do understanding and generation share an encoder? (shared vs. decoupled). The answers define four major architectural families.

Taxonomy of Unified Understanding & Generation Architectures

Approach 1: Shared Backbone with Discrete Tokens

The conceptually simplest approach: convert everything — text, images, video — into discrete tokens using VQ-VAE, then train a single transformer with standard next-token prediction. One vocabulary, one loss function, one attention pattern.

Chameleon Meta, May 2024

First large-scale early-fusion model trained from scratch on interleaved mixed-modal data. 7B/34B parameters with a unified vocabulary of 73,728 tokens (65K text + 8K image). Critical contribution: solving training instability via QK-Norm and z-loss regularization — without which training diverges after ~20% of an epoch. The image tokenizer encodes 512×512 images into 1,024 discrete tokens.

Emu3 BAAI, Sep 2024 — Nature 2025

The philosophically purest approach: next-token prediction is all you need. No diffusion, no special attention patterns, no dual losses. Uses a 32,768-entry codebook (MoVQGAN) producing 4,096 tokens per 512×512 image. Separate post-training produces Emu3-Gen (image generation) and Emu3-Chat (understanding) variants, hinting that full task unification still requires specialization.

⚠

The Stability Challenge

Mixed-modal early-fusion training is notoriously unstable. Different modalities have vastly different entropy distributions, causing the softmax function to behave poorly — logit norms grow uncontrollably, leading to divergence. Chameleon's QK-Norm, z-loss, and output norm monitoring are essential techniques that all discrete-token omni models must address.

Approach 2: Autoregressive + Diffusion Hybrid

Instead of discretizing images (which loses information), keep images in continuous latent space and train a single transformer with two different loss functions: cross-entropy for text and diffusion loss for image patches. This avoids the quantization bottleneck entirely.

Transfusion Meta, Aug 2024

Text tokens use causal attention with cross-entropy loss. Image patches use bidirectional attention within each image with diffusion loss (MSE). Both coexist in a single transformer, with modality-specific delimiters (BOI/EOI) triggering mode switches. At 7B/2T tokens, matches Llama-1 on text while achieving COCO FID of 6.78. 34× more compute-efficient than Chameleon for equivalent image generation quality.

Show-o NUS + ByteDance, Aug 2024

A unique middle ground: both text and images are discrete tokens, but they use different generation mechanisms. Text uses autoregressive (left-to-right) prediction. Images use discrete diffusion (MaskGIT-style iterative unmasking in ~8 steps vs. 256 sequential AR steps). An “omni-attention” mechanism adapts attention patterns per task: causal for text, full bidirectional for images.

Intuition: Why bidirectional attention matters for images

Language is inherently sequential — each word depends on the words before it. But images are inherently spatial — every pixel relates to every other pixel simultaneously. Forcing images through purely causal (left-to-right) attention is like describing a painting one pixel at a time without being able to look at the whole picture. Transfusion's bidirectional intra-image attention lets every image patch see every other patch, enabling spatial coherence. This single change gives a 3× improvement in FID (61.3 → 20.3).

Approach 3: Decoupled Visual Encoding

Janus's central insight: understanding and generation need different visual representations, but they can share the same language model backbone. Instead of forcing a single visual encoder to serve both tasks, use separate encoders that are each optimized for their own purpose.

Approach 4: Composition-Based Routing

The most modular approach: use an LLM as a central controller that routes signals to frozen specialist encoders and decoders for each modality. This enables any-to-any generation across text, images, video, and audio without training everything from scratch.

The Convergence Pattern

Despite their differences, these architectures are converging. The emerging consensus for 2025 is: a single transformer backbone with modality-specific lightweight heads, using either decoupled encoders (Janus pattern) or dual-loss training (Transfusion pattern) to resolve the understanding-generation tension. Discrete Token Models

Models that tokenize everything — text, images, video — into discrete tokens and use standard next-token prediction for both understanding and generation.

The Core Idea

The simplest path to unifying understanding and generation: convert every modality into discrete tokens and train a single transformer with a single objective — next-token prediction via cross-entropy loss. Text already lives in token space (BPE). Images and video are discretized through a visual tokenizer (VQ-VAE, VQGAN, or similar). Once everything is tokens, the model treats an interleaved sequence of text and image tokens identically.

Intuition: One vocabulary to rule them all

Imagine expanding a language model’s vocabulary from ~65K text tokens to ~74K tokens by adding 8,192 “image words.” Each image word represents a small visual pattern from a learned codebook. A 512×512 image becomes a sentence of 1,024 image words. Now the model can read and write in both languages — English and “visual” — using the exact same next-word prediction mechanism it already knows.

💡

Why Discrete?

Discrete tokens are a natural fit for language models: the same cross-entropy loss, the same softmax output head, the same autoregressive sampling. No architectural changes are needed — you simply expand the vocabulary. This means all existing LLM infrastructure (distributed training, KV caching, speculative decoding) works out of the box.

Emu3 — Next-Token Prediction Is All You Need

Emu3  |  BAAI, Sep 2024  |  Published in Nature (2025)  |  arXiv 2409.18869

The philosophically purest approach: everything is tokens, and next-token prediction is the only objective. No diffusion, no special attention patterns, no dual losses. Images, text, and video are all discrete tokens processed by a single transformer with standard cross-entropy loss. The bet: a sufficiently good visual tokenizer + enough scale makes the simpler architecture win.

~8.5B params  |  SBER-MoVQGAN tokenizer (32,768 codebook)  |  4,096 tokens per 512×512 image  |  unified vocab ~184,622

Architecture Details

Emu3 uses a decoder-only transformer with 32 layers, 4,096 hidden dimensions, 32 attention heads with 8 KV heads (grouped-query attention), SiLU activation, and RoPE positional embeddings. The maximum sequence length is 9,216 tokens. The text tokenizer is Qwen-based BPE with 151K+ tokens.

💡

The Tokenizer Is the Key

Emu3 invests heavily in its visual tokenizer (Emu3-VisionTokenizer) rather than in architectural complexity. Based on SBER-MoVQGAN, it uses a 32,768-entry codebook — 4× larger than Chameleon’s — with 4-dimensional embeddings and 8×8 spatial compression. It also supports variable resolutions and video (via temporal downsampling at 4×).

Training

Two-stage pre-training: Stage 1 trains on text + images at 5,120 context length from scratch. Stage 2 introduces video and expands context to 131,072 tokens. The loss is pure cross-entropy — no diffusion objective, no separate losses. Post-training produces two variants: Emu3-Gen (optimized for image generation) and Emu3-Chat (optimized for vision-language understanding).

⚠

The specialization tax

Despite the unified pre-training, Emu3 requires separate post-training variants for generation vs. understanding. This reveals the underlying tension: a single set of weights struggles to be optimal at both tasks simultaneously, even with the simplest possible architecture.

Unified-IO 2 — One Model, 220+ Tasks, 7 Modalities

Unified-IO 2  |  AI2 (Allen Institute), 2024  |  arXiv 2312.17172

The most ambitious scope: a single autoregressive model that handles 220+ tasks across 7 modalities (images, text, audio, video, 3D, action tokens, bounding boxes). Uses dynamic packing of different modalities into a shared sequence, with modality-specific tokenizers feeding a unified encoder-decoder transformer.

7B params  |  Encoder-decoder architecture  |  7 modalities  |  1B+ training examples across 220+ tasks

Architecture

Unified-IO 2 uses an encoder-decoder transformer (not decoder-only like the others). Each modality has its own tokenizer: ViT for image encoding, spectrogram + AST for audio, point-cloud tokenizer for 3D. All tokens are projected into a shared embedding space and packed into the encoder input. The decoder autoregressively generates output tokens that are routed to modality-specific de-tokenizers.

Its breadth is remarkable — a single set of weights handles image generation, image understanding, VQA, object detection, depth estimation, audio captioning, action prediction, surface normal estimation, and more. However, this breadth comes at a cost: it does not match specialist models on any single task.

Advantages & Disadvantages

Advantages

  • Architectural simplicity: One transformer, one loss function, one vocabulary. Standard LLM training infrastructure (distributed training, KV caching, FSDP) works unchanged.
  • Shared representations: Text and image tokens share the same embedding space, enabling emergent cross-modal reasoning and interleaved generation.
  • Scaling is straightforward: More parameters, more data, more compute — the LLM scaling recipe applies directly. Emu3’s Nature publication validates this path.
  • Natural interleaving: Mixed text-image sequences are trivial — tokens are just tokens. No special routing or modality-switching logic needed.

Disadvantages

  • Codebook bottleneck: Image quality is fundamentally bounded by the visual tokenizer’s codebook. Even 32K codes cannot capture all visual nuances — fine textures, subtle gradients, and text rendering suffer.
  • Generation quality lags: Emu3 (GenEval 0.54) trails Janus-Pro (0.80) and dedicated diffusion models (SD3: 0.68). The discrete bottleneck is real.
  • Training instability: Mixed-modal softmax causes entropy conflicts. Chameleon needed QK-Norm, z-loss, and careful monitoring to avoid divergence.
  • Long sequences: 4,096 tokens per image (Emu3) or 1,024 (Chameleon) means video generation requires enormous context lengths.

Diffusion Head Models

Models that combine autoregressive text generation with diffusion-based image generation in a single forward pass. The best of both paradigms.

The Key Insight

Text is naturally discrete. Images are naturally continuous. Why force images into a discrete codebook when doing so loses information? Diffusion-head models embrace this asymmetry: they keep text tokens discrete (processed with cross-entropy loss) while keeping image representations continuous (processed with diffusion or masking loss). A single transformer handles both, switching between objectives based on modality.

Intuition: Bilingual with two grammars

Think of the model as a bilingual speaker fluent in two very different languages. For “text language,” it speaks one word at a time, left-to-right (autoregressive). For “image language,” it starts with a rough sketch (noise) and progressively refines it everywhere at once (diffusion). The same brain handles both, but uses different strategies for each. This is more natural than forcing images to speak in “text grammar” via discrete codebooks.

Transfusion — Predict and Diffuse with One Model

Transfusion  |  Meta AI / Waymo / USC, Aug 2024  |  arXiv 2408.11039

Transfusion makes a radical architectural choice: images remain in continuous latent space and are processed with a diffusion loss, while text uses standard next-token prediction. A single transformer handles both, with modality-specific attention masking and loss functions. This avoids the quantization bottleneck entirely.

Up to 7B + 0.27B U-Net overhead  |  Custom 86M VAE (8-dim latent)  |  Trained on 2T tokens  |  λ = 5 diffusion upweight

Architecture

The input is a mixed sequence of discrete text tokens and continuous image patch vectors. Special BOI/EOI delimiters mark image boundaries. The transformer applies causal attention across the full sequence (maintaining autoregressive ordering) plus bidirectional attention within each image (every patch in an image can attend to every other patch in the same image). Text tokens and patches from other images can only be attended to if they appeared earlier.

Transfusion: Mixed Discrete + Continuous Sequence

Image Encoding & Patch Compression

Transfusion uses a custom 86M-parameter VAE (CNN encoder/decoder, 8-dimensional latent space) that compresses 256×256 images to 32×32×8 latent tensors. These are then patchified: with 2×2 patches, each image becomes 256 continuous vectors; with 8×8 patches, just 16 vectors per image. Each patch is a continuous vector, never quantized.

💡

16 patches per image — and it still works

With U-Net encoding and 8×8 patches, Transfusion compresses each image to just 16 continuous vectors while maintaining FID ~16 on MS-COCO. This is a 64× reduction in sequence length compared to 1,024-token discrete approaches, with enormous implications for serving efficiency and video generation.

Show-o — Autoregressive + Discrete Diffusion

Show-o  |  Show Lab (NUS) + ByteDance, Aug 2024  |  ICLR 2025  |  arXiv 2408.12528

Show-o introduces a unique hybrid: text is generated autoregressively (left-to-right), but images are generated via discrete diffusion (MaskGIT-style iterative unmasking). Both happen within a single transformer through an “omni-attention” mechanism that adapts the attention pattern per task.

1.3B params (Phi-1.5 backbone)  |  MAGVIT-v2 tokenizer (8,192 codebook, 256 tokens/image)  |  ~8 denoising steps for images

Omni-Attention Mechanism

The attention pattern adapts dynamically per task:

  • Multimodal understanding: Text uses causal attention; text tokens can attend to all image tokens (full context for reasoning).
  • Text-to-image generation: Image tokens use full bidirectional attention among themselves and can attend to all text tokens. Text tokens use causal attention.
  • Text-only: Degrades to standard causal attention (a pure language model).

Discrete Diffusion Process

Instead of continuous Gaussian noise, Show-o uses discrete masking. During training, image tokens are randomly replaced with [MASK] tokens at varying rates. During generation:

  1. Start with all 256 image positions set to [MASK]
  2. The model predicts original tokens for all masked positions simultaneously
  3. Keep the highest-confidence predictions; re-mask the lowest-confidence ones
  4. Repeat for T steps (typically T = 8)

This is roughly 32× fewer sequential steps than autoregressive generation (which would need 256 steps for a 16×16 image grid), while still producing all tokens as discrete codes.

Why Diffusion Heads Over Pure Discrete?

No Codebook Bottleneck

Transfusion keeps images in continuous latent space — no quantization, no information loss. The model can represent arbitrarily fine visual details without being limited by a finite codebook. This is why Transfusion achieves FID 6.78 while Chameleon gets 29.6 at the same scale.

Right Tool for Each Modality

Cross-entropy is the natural objective for text (discrete vocabulary, sequential structure). Diffusion/masking is the natural objective for images (continuous signals, spatial coherence). Using both respects the inherent structure of each modality rather than forcing a lowest-common-denominator approach.

Compute Efficiency

Transfusion matches Chameleon’s image quality at 2.9% of the FLOPs. Show-o generates images in ~8 steps instead of 256+ autoregressive steps. The right inductive bias means less compute is wasted fighting the representation mismatch.

Extreme Compression Possible

Transfusion can represent an image as just 16 continuous vectors (8×8 patches) while maintaining competitive FID. This makes video generation feasible where discrete approaches would require tens of thousands of tokens per second of video.

⚠

The trade-off: increased complexity

Diffusion-head models are architecturally more complex than pure discrete approaches. They require dual loss functions, hybrid attention masks, separate inference modes (autoregressive for text, iterative denoising for images), and careful balancing of the loss weight λ. Show-o adds a third dimension: task-specific prompt tokens ([MMU], [T2I]) to route the model’s behavior. This complexity may limit adoption compared to the “just predict the next token” simplicity of Emu3.

The Representation Conflict

All unified models face a fundamental tension: understanding and generation want different things from a visual encoder. Understanding needs high-level semantic features — abstract representations that capture what is in an image. Generation needs fine-grained spatial features — precise representations that capture exactly how every pixel looks.

⚠

The Core Problem

A semantic encoder like SigLIP compresses a 384×384 image into features that are excellent for answering “What breed is this dog?” but discard the spatial detail needed to reconstruct that exact dog. Conversely, a VQ-VAE preserves pixel-level structure but lacks the abstract reasoning features needed for visual question answering. No single encoder can optimally serve both tasks.

Why This Matters Going Forward

Decoupled encoding is not just a performance trick — it reveals a structural truth about multimodal AI. Understanding and generation are fundamentally different cognitive processes that require different information. The analogy extends beyond vision: audio understanding benefits from spectral features while audio generation needs waveform-level detail. Any future omni-model that handles N modalities may need 2N encoder pathways (one for understanding, one for generation per modality) feeding into a single shared reasoning backbone. The backbone is the unifier; the encoders are the specialists.

What “Any-to-Any” Means

Any-to-any generation is the most ambitious form of multimodal AI: a single system that accepts arbitrary combinations of modalities as input (text, image, audio, video) and produces arbitrary combinations as output. Unlike specialist models (text → image) or even multimodal LLMs (image + text → text only), any-to-any models handle the full N×N matrix of modality transformations.

The Composition Bottleneck

Because the diffusion decoders are frozen, NExT-GPT’s generation quality is capped by the capabilities of its external decoders (Stable Diffusion v1.5 era, AudioLDM, Zeroscope). There is no joint optimization between the LLM and generation modules. This is the fundamental limitation of composition-based approaches — and the motivation for native multimodal models.

CoDi / CoDi-2: Composable Diffusion

CoDi Microsoft, NeurIPS 2023

The first model to generate arbitrary combinations of modalities simultaneously. Entirely diffusion-based (no LLM backbone): independent modality-specific latent diffusion models connected through a “bridging alignment” that projects all latent spaces into a shared cross-modal space. Achieves synchronized video + audio generation with temporal coherence.

CoDi-2 Microsoft, CVPR 2024

Adds an LLM backbone to CoDi’s diffusion architecture, enabling in-context learning, multi-turn editing, and zero-shot/few-shot reasoning over multimodal instructions. Trained with both pixel loss (diffusion) and token loss (language modeling). Bridges the gap between pure generation (CoDi) and reasoning (LLMs).

Bridging Alignment: Avoiding N² Training

The key challenge in any-to-any generation is combinatorial: with 4 modalities, there are 12 directed modality pairs to align. CoDi’s elegant solution is bridging alignment:

  1. Phase 1: Train each modality-specific LDM independently with high-quality modality-specific data
  2. Phase 2: Align each modality with a single “bridge” modality (text) using contrastive learning
  3. Phase 3: Transitivity provides alignment across all modality pairs — including combinations never seen during training

At inference, multiple LDMs run simultaneously with cross-attention between their denoising processes, enabling synchronized joint generation (e.g., temporally aligned video + matching audio).

📍

CoDi’s Limitation

CoDi is purely generative — it has no language understanding or reasoning capability. It cannot perform VQA, captioning, or any comprehension task. This is what CoDi-2 addresses by adding an LLM backbone, but at the cost of significantly more complex training.

The Native Generation Era

Between late 2024 and early 2026, a decisive shift occurred at the frontier: every major AI lab began shipping models that natively generate images within the same architecture that handles text understanding. GPT-4o & GPT-Image-1 (OpenAI)

GPT-4o was the model that brought native omni generation to the mainstream. Released as a multimodal model in May 2024, it gained native image generation in March 2025, replacing the previous ChatGPT + DALL-E pipeline with an integrated system where image creation occurs inside the model itself.

GPT-4o — Native Image Generation

Architecture: Transfusion (AR text + diffusion images in one transformer)

Input: Text, images, audio  |  Output: Text, images, audio

API variants: GPT-Image-1 (Apr 2025) → GPT-Image-1 Mini (Oct 2025) → GPT-Image-1.5 (Dec 2025, 20% cost reduction)

Key strength: Best-in-class text rendering within generated images, compositional control over 10–20 objects, conversational multi-turn editing

GPT-4o’s image generation is based on the Transfusion architecture: a single decoder-only transformer processes interleaved discrete text tokens and continuous image patch embeddings. Text tokens use standard next-token prediction (cross-entropy loss), while image patches use a diffusion denoising objective (L2 loss) — both losses are summed during training. This hybrid approach avoids the quantization bottleneck of discrete image tokens while preserving the autoregressive framework for text.

Why Transfusion excels at text rendering

Earlier diffusion models struggle with text in images because they generate all pixels simultaneously through denoising — they have no sequential concept of character order. GPT-4o’s autoregressive backbone processes the text prompt token-by-token, building a sequential understanding of what characters appear and where. The diffusion head then renders the image conditioned on this rich sequential representation. The result: accurate multi-word text rendering that previous image generators could not achieve.

At launch, GPT-4o’s image generation debuted in equal first place on the Artificial Analysis Image Arena (ELO-based), outperforming Recraft V3, FLUX 1.1 Pro, and all other competitors. Its ability to leverage world knowledge during generation — generating historically accurate scenes, following complex multi-step instructions — demonstrated the power of true understanding-generation unification.

The Convergence Trend

The evidence is now overwhelming: every major AI lab is adding native generation to their flagship models. This is not a niche research direction — it is the new default architecture for frontier AI systems. Several forces are driving this convergence simultaneously.

Why Every Lab Is Going Native

Shared representations improve reasoning: When understanding and generation share the same latent space, the model can use world knowledge during generation — producing historically accurate scenes, following complex multi-step instructions, and maintaining consistency across modalities.

Scaling laws favor unified models: At hundreds of billions of parameters, maintaining separate specialist models for each modality is prohibitively expensive. A single unified model amortizes compute across tasks and enables emergent cross-modal capabilities not present in specialist systems.

Product demand for conversational creativity: Users expect to say “make it more blue,” “add a hat,” “now show me the same scene at night.” This requires simultaneous understanding, reasoning, and generation — exactly what native omni models provide.

The Transfusion breakthrough proved feasibility: Meta’s 2024 paper demonstrated that joint training with autoregressive + diffusion losses works at scale, with no degradation in either modality. This removed the key technical objection.

⚠

Specialist models are not dead (yet)

For raw generation quality in narrow domains, specialist models like Midjourney, FLUX, and Recraft V3 still lead on photorealism, style diversity, and fine-grained design control. The frontier omni models are rapidly closing this gap — GPT-4o’s image generation matched the best specialists on overall preference — but for tasks like professional typography, vector output, or extreme photorealism, dedicated generators retain an edge. The question is whether this gap persists at the next scale.

Looking ahead, several trajectories seem clear. All frontier LLMs will support native image generation by end of 2026. Video generation will follow — Movie Gen and Show-o2 have already demonstrated the path. Audio and speech integration (Qwen3-Omni, Step-Audio) is becoming standard. The distinction between “understanding model” and “generation model” is disappearing at the frontier, replaced by omni models that do both.

Interleaved Multimodal Pretraining

The foundational insight behind unified model training: rather than treating images and text as separate paired datasets (one caption per image), train on interleaved multimodal sequences — long documents where text, images, and video are naturally woven together, the way content appears on a web page, in a textbook, or on Wikipedia.

Why interleaving matters

Consider a Wikipedia article about the Eiffel Tower. It contains text paragraphs, photographs from different angles, construction diagrams, and historical images — all in a meaningful sequence. A model trained on such interleaved documents learns to use earlier images as context for understanding later text, to track cross-modal references, and to generate images that are contextually appropriate to surrounding text. Paired (image, caption) training cannot teach any of this.

A typical interleaved training sequence uses special tokens to demarcate image boundaries:

Interleaved Sequence Format

[TEXT] The Eiffel Tower is a wrought-iron lattice tower in Paris.

[BOI] <image_tokens_1> [EOI]

[TEXT] It was constructed from 1887 to 1889 as the centerpiece of the World’s Fair.

[BOI] <image_tokens_2> [EOI]

[TEXT] The tower is 330 metres tall...

BOI/EOI = Begin/End of Image. Text tokens use causal attention. Image tokens may use bidirectional attention within their span (Transfusion), full attention (Show-o), or standard autoregressive ordering (Chameleon, Emu3).

Mixed Objective Training

The central technical challenge: how do you train a single model with fundamentally different objectives for text (discrete next-token prediction) and images (continuous denoising)? Two major paradigms have emerged.

Paradigm A: Unified Next-Token Prediction

All modalities discretized into tokens from a shared vocabulary. A single cross-entropy loss applies everywhere.

Models: Chameleon, Emu3, VILA-U

Advantage: Architectural simplicity — one loss, one model, one training loop. Disadvantage: discrete tokenization introduces information loss; generation quality depends on tokenizer reconstruction quality.

Paradigm B: AR + Diffusion (Transfusion)

Different objectives for different modalities within the same forward pass: cross-entropy for text, MSE/diffusion loss for image patches.

Models: Transfusion, Show-o, MetaMorph

Advantage: Each modality optimized with its natural objective. Disadvantage: requires careful loss weighting; two distinct training signals through shared parameters.

Loss Weighting Strategies

The choice of how to weight different modality losses has an outsized impact on final model quality. Without careful balancing, one loss can dominate gradients and the model effectively ignores the other modality.

Why Weighting Is Critical

The natural scales of the two losses differ enormously. Cross-entropy for text typically ranges from 1–4 nats for well-trained models. MSE for diffusion can vary by orders of magnitude depending on the noise schedule and normalization. Transfusion sets λ = 5, weighting the diffusion loss 5× higher — reflecting the greater difficulty of the image generation task. The authors acknowledged this value as “suboptimal” and deferred further tuning.

Alternative approaches: Chameleon and Emu3 sidestep the weighting problem entirely by using a single cross-entropy loss over all token types — but then the tokenizer quality becomes the bottleneck. Show-o uses discrete diffusion (mask prediction) which produces a cross-entropy-like loss, making weighting more natural.

The Seesaw Effect

The most pernicious challenge in unified model training: improving one modality can degrade the other. This empirical phenomenon — called the seesaw effect — has been systematically documented across multiple research groups.

⚠

Evidence from GapEval (2025)

A large portion of test samples can only be correctly answered in one modality (understanding OR generation, not both). Knowledge editing focused on understanding produced 0.62 improvement for BAGEL but near-zero generation gains. Generation-focused training (0.89 improvement for Show-o) left understanding largely unchanged. Despite unified architectures, the models store understanding and generation knowledge in largely separate parameter subspaces.

Asymmetric Convergence

Understanding tasks improve rapidly, saturating around 0.8 accuracy after minimal training steps. Generation progresses substantially slower. This creates temporary optimization conflicts where gradients pull shared parameters in opposing directions.

Entropy Mismatch

Text tokens have low entropy (predictable distributions). Image tokens have high entropy (variable distributions). When mixed in the same softmax, each modality “competes” by increasing its norms, causing slow norm growth that can eventually diverge (documented by Chameleon at scale).

Knowledge Disjointness

Despite sharing weights, the model stores understanding and generation knowledge in different parameter subspaces. Fine-tuning one capability fails to improve the other — the knowledge representations remain “largely decoupled.”

Curriculum Learning

Nearly all successful unified models employ staged training where modalities, data quality levels, and training objectives are introduced progressively. The general principle: text first, then images, then video; simple data before complex; low quality before high quality.

Unified Model Training Pipeline
Janus’s Three-Stage Curriculum (Representative Example)

Stage I — Adaptor Training: Visual encoders and LLM are frozen. Only adaptors and the image prediction head are trained on 1.25M image-text pairs + 1.2M ImageNet samples. Purpose: establish embedding connections between vision and language.

Stage II — Unified Pretraining: LLM unfrozen; all parameters trainable. Mixed data including text-only, interleaved image-text (WikiHow, WIT), captions, tables/charts, and generation data. ImageNet shown first (120K steps) to learn basic pixel dependencies, then other datasets for 60K steps. Data ratio: 2:3:5 (understanding : text : generation).

Stage III — Supervised Fine-tuning: Instruction-tuning data with ratio 7:3:10 (text : understanding : generation). 4M additional in-house generation data. Generation-heavy ratios in later stages reflect the asymmetry: understanding transfers well from the pretrained LLM, while generation must be learned from scratch.

Data Requirements

Training unified models demands three categories of data, each serving a distinct purpose and each with its own quality requirements.

Interleaved Image-Text

Web-crawled documents with naturally interspersed text and images. Enables in-context learning and cross-modal reference tracking.

Key dataset: OBELICS (141M web pages, 353M images, 115B text tokens). Filtering: HTML DOM analysis, perplexity scoring, image deduplication (84.3% unique).

High-Quality Captions

Detailed image descriptions far beyond typical alt-text. Generation quality is highly sensitive to caption quality — 95% synthetic captions can outperform raw web captions.

Key dataset: ShareGPT4V (1.2M GPT-4V-level descriptions). Modern practice: recaption all training images with a strong VLM.

Video Data

Temporal sequences that introduce motion, causality, and scene dynamics. Massive token requirements (a 10-second clip at Emu3 tokenization = ~80K tokens).

Processing: Scene detection (PySceneDetect), optical flow filtering, text overlay removal, temporal sampling at 2–4 fps. Introduced in later training stages only.

Alignment: SFT and RLHF/DPO for Both Tasks

Post-training alignment for unified models must address both understanding quality (accurate, helpful responses about images) and generation quality (aesthetically pleasing, prompt-following images). This creates unique challenges absent in text-only alignment.

Diffusion-DPO (CVPR 2024)

Key idea: Adapt Direct Preference Optimization to diffusion models by reformulating the DPO objective to account for diffusion likelihood via the evidence lower bound. Trained on the Pick-a-Pic dataset of 851K crowdsourced pairwise preferences.

Result: Fine-tuned SDXL-1.0 significantly outperforms both the base model and the larger SDXL refinement model in human evaluation. A variant using AI feedback achieves comparable performance to human preferences, enabling scalable alignment.

Understanding-as-Critic (UiG, 2025)

Key idea: Use the unified model’s own understanding capability as a critic to improve its generation. The pipeline: (1) generate an initial image, (2) use the understanding head to verify and critique it, (3) incorporate feedback into editing instructions, (4) iteratively improve. Result: 3.92% gain on the TIIF benchmark for long-prompt generation.

A related approach, SUDER (2025), uses the model’s own understanding and generation as dual self-reward signals, enabling self-improvement without external feedback.

⚠

The Multimodal DPO Trap

Standard DPO in multimodal settings can suffer from unconditional preferences — the model learns to prefer certain text outputs regardless of the input image, effectively ignoring the visual modality during optimization. MDPO (EMNLP 2024) addresses this with conditional preference optimization that forces the model to attend to visual content when forming preferences.

The core asymmetry in unified training

Understanding capabilities transfer well from the pretrained text LLM — the model already knows language, and learning to interpret images builds on top of that foundation. Generation, however, must be learned largely from scratch: predicting pixel-level details, spatial layouts, and visual textures has no direct equivalent in text pretraining. This asymmetry explains why every successful training recipe allocates disproportionate data and compute to generation in later stages (Janus: 50% generation data in Stage III), and why curriculum learning — text first, images second, video third — consistently outperforms joint training from the start.

Scaling Laws Token Efficiency Quality Gap Convergence

1. Do Unified Models Follow Text-Only Scaling Laws?

Short answer: yes, with important caveats. Multimodal unified models follow power-law scaling between compute (FLOPs), model size, and validation loss — the same fundamental behaviour observed in text-only LLMs. But the exponents, coefficients, and optimal resource allocations differ from the text-only regime.

Intuition: Why Scaling Laws Matter Here

If unified models didn't follow predictable scaling laws, we would have no principled way to allocate compute between understanding and generation. The fact that they do means we can predict performance gains before committing billions of GPU-hours — and decide the optimal split between text tokens, image tokens, and model parameters.

Apple's Scaling Study: 457 Trained Models (ICCV 2025)

The most comprehensive multimodal scaling analysis to date, from Apple and Sorbonne University. Key findings:

  • Power-law scaling holds for both early-fusion and late-fusion native multimodal models
  • Early fusion matches late fusion at the same compute budget, but with different trade-offs: early-fusion uses fewer parameters and more tokens; late-fusion uses more parameters and fewer tokens
  • Sparse MoE is highly effective: modality-agnostic routing consistently outperforms both dense models and modality-aware routing
  • Scaling exponents: sparse early-fusion follows power-law exponents similar to dense models (−0.047 vs −0.049) but with a smaller multiplicative constant (26.3 vs 29.6)

Bottom line: Compute-optimal models should scale training tokens faster than parameters as the budget grows — the same Chinchilla insight, extended to multimodal.

Chameleon's Scaling Analysis (Meta, 2024)

Chameleon provides the most detailed account of the challenges that appear when you scale unified models to tens of billions of parameters.

  • Softmax instability: Text tokens have low-entropy distributions; image tokens have high-entropy distributions. When mixed in the same softmax, each modality competes by increasing its norms — causing slow divergence after 20–30% of training
  • QK-Norm is essential: Without it, Chameleon-7B diverges after ~20% of one epoch. With it, training proceeds stably to 4.4T tokens
  • Scaling requires different stabilization at different sizes: 7B needed QK-Norm + dropout (0.1) + z-loss; 34B needed QK-Norm + z-loss + reordered layer norm (no dropout)
Emu3's Scaling Observations (BAAI, 2024)

Emu3 documented clear power-law behaviour for three separate tasks:

  • Text-to-image: Validation loss decreases as a power law of both model size and training tokens
  • Image-to-text: Same power-law behaviour with consistent exponents
  • Text-to-video: Also follows power-law scaling, extending the paradigm beyond static images

This confirms that next-token prediction on a unified token space enables standard LLM-like scaling properties even for generation tasks.

5. Where They Fall Short

High-Resolution Photorealism

Specialist diffusion models (FLUX, Midjourney v6, DALL-E 3) still produce more photorealistic outputs at resolutions above 512×512. Most unified models train at 256×256 or 384×384 — scaling to 1024+ multiplies token counts and compute requirements dramatically.

Complex Visual Reasoning

Top specialist understanding models (GPT-4o, Claude 3.5 Sonnet) significantly outperform current unified models on multi-step inference, document understanding, and OCR-heavy tasks. Chameleon notes: “a core weakness of our tokenizer is in reconstructing images with a large amount of text.”

Surface-Level Unification

The GapEval benchmark (2025) reveals that current models achieve “surface-level unification rather than deep cognitive convergence.” Models store understanding and generation knowledge in largely separate parameter subspaces — fine-tuning on one modality fails to improve the other.

1. Open Challenges

Despite rapid progress, unified omni models face fundamental challenges that limit their reliability, quality, and deployability. These problems range from deep architectural issues to societal concerns.

⚠ Multi-Modal Hallucination

Unified models inherit LLM hallucination tendencies and amplify them across modalities. A model may generate an image that contradicts its own textual description, produce visual details that don’t exist in the source material, or confidently describe objects in an image that aren’t present. The GapEval benchmark (2025) found that even frontier models achieve only ~59.65% accuracy on common-sense QA tasks requiring both correct image generation and accurate reasoning.

Cross-modal consistency remains the deepest challenge. Hallucination in one modality propagates to others through shared representations.

⚠ Generation Quality Gap

While the gap on prompt-following benchmarks (GenEval) has closed, specialist models still lead on photorealism at high resolution (1024×1024+), fine-grained details (text rendering, complex compositions), and generation diversity / style control. Most unified models train at 256–384px — scaling to higher resolutions multiplies token counts quadratically.

Resolution scaling is compute-bound: a 1024×1024 image requires 4–16× more tokens than 512×512.

⚠ Compute Efficiency

Unified models face a triple compute burden: (1) the model must be large enough to handle both understanding and generation, (2) image/video tokens dominate context windows (a 10-second video clip can require 80K+ tokens), and (3) diffusion-based generation requires iterative denoising steps. Training Chameleon-34B consumed ~4.4T tokens on massive GPU clusters. Even inference is expensive — generating a single image with Transfusion requires 250 diffusion steps within the transformer.

MoE architectures (Gemini, Aurora) help decouple capacity from per-token cost, but this remains a frontier challenge.

⚠ Evaluation Frameworks

Existing benchmarks evaluate understanding and generation separately. GenEval measures prompt-following but not visual quality or photorealism. FID captures distribution similarity but not instruction adherence. The MME-Unify benchmark (ICLR 2026) is a first attempt at holistic evaluation, but the field still lacks consensus on how to measure true cross-modal unification — the ability to apply knowledge consistently across modalities.

GapEval (2025) showed that high scores on separate benchmarks do not imply genuine cross-modal integration.

⚠ Video Generation Integration

Extending unified models from images to video introduces massive token overhead (4,096+ tokens per second at 2 FPS), temporal consistency requirements that image-only training doesn’t address, and audio-visual synchronization challenges. Specialist video models (Sora, Veo 3, Runway Gen-3) remain far ahead. Only Emu3 and LWM have demonstrated video generation within a unified framework, with substantially lower quality than dedicated systems.

Video-as-tokens requires context windows of 100K+ for even short clips, pushing hardware limits.

⚠ Safety & Misuse

Unified models that both understand and generate images create novel safety risks: realistic deepfakes generated from a single reference photo, automated creation of misleading content grounded in real-world knowledge, and potential for instruction-following systems to bypass safety filters through multi-turn manipulation. The combination of world knowledge (understanding) with creative capability (generation) makes these models more potent for both beneficial and harmful uses than either capability alone.

Chameleon dedicates 95.3K safety SFT samples; GPT-4o uses extensive RLHF-based guardrails. Neither approach is complete.

The Seesaw Effect

⚠
The seesaw effect remains a fundamental architectural challenge: improving generation performance can degrade understanding performance, and vice versa. GapEval (2025) found that the gap initially widens at moderate capability levels before narrowing at higher performance tiers. Fine-tuning on one modality fails to generalize to the other, revealing that the two capabilities rely on distinct knowledge representations within shared parameters.

Root causes include: (1) asymmetric convergence — understanding saturates around 0.8 accuracy quickly while generation progresses much slower, (2) gradient interference — gradients from the two tasks can conflict in shared parameters, and (3) entropy mismatch — text tokens have low entropy while image tokens have high entropy, causing softmax instability.

2. Future Directions

The convergence toward unified omni models is accelerating. These directions represent the most transformative research frontiers for 2025–2027.

World Models: Unified Understanding + Generation + Action

The ultimate goal is a model that doesn’t just understand and generate static content, but simulates interactive environments. Genie 3 (DeepMind, 2026) demonstrates real-time interactive world generation at 20–24 FPS. The convergence of video generation (Sora, Veo 3) with unified understanding (GPT-4o, Gemini) points toward models that can reason about, predict, and interact with dynamic environments. This unites computer vision, language understanding, and reinforcement learning into a single architecture — the vision of Ha & Schmidhuber (2018) realized at scale.

Real-Time Multimodal Interaction

Qwen3-Omni achieves 211ms audio-only and 507ms audio-video latency with streaming speech output. Step-Audio (130B params) unifies speech recognition, understanding, and synthesis. The trajectory points toward omni models that participate in real-time conversations with full multimodal fluency — seeing, hearing, speaking, and creating images or videos within a continuous interactive loop. GPT-4o’s conversational image editing (multi-turn “make it more blue” / “add a hat” workflows) demonstrates early product-level realization of this vision.

Video as a First-Class Citizen

Current unified models treat video as a sequence of image tokens, but dedicated architectures are emerging. Show-o2 extends the unified framework with 3D Causal VAE and spatial-temporal fusion. Movie Gen (Meta, 30B params) applies Flow Matching in a Transformer backbone. The key challenge is making video generation efficient enough to be unified: LWM demonstrates that RingAttention can handle 1M-token sequences (hour-long video), but generation at that scale remains impractical. Innovations in temporal compression (LLaMA-VID’s 2 tokens/frame), keyframe-based approaches, and streaming architectures will be essential.

Democratization of Omni Models

The open-source ecosystem is rapidly closing the gap with proprietary systems. Key milestones: BAGEL (ByteDance, 7B active/14B total, fully open), Janus-Pro (DeepSeek, MIT license), NextStep-1.1 (StepFun, open weights), and BLIP3-o (Salesforce, fully open). Janus demonstrated competitive performance with only 7 days on 128 A100 GPUs — within reach of academic labs. The next wave will bring omni capabilities to models small enough for edge deployment, following the trajectory of LLM democratization (LLaMA → Mistral → Phi).

💡
The 2026 outlook: All frontier LLMs are expected to support native image generation by end of 2026. Video generation will follow the same path. Audio/speech integration is becoming standard. The distinction between “understanding model” and “generation model” is rapidly disappearing at the frontier, replaced by omni models that do both.
