Agentic Video Generation: LLM-Orchestrated Visual Production

Large language models are becoming the directors, editors, and producers of video content — decomposing complex creative briefs into structured plans, coordinating specialized generation models, and orchestrating end-to-end visual production pipelines with iterative refinement.

What Is Agentic Video Generation?

Traditional video generation follows a single-model paradigm: you write a text prompt, feed it to a diffusion transformer, and hope the output matches your intent. Agentic video generation represents a fundamental paradigm shift. Instead of relying on one monolithic model to handle everything, an LLM agent serves as the central “brain” that plans, reasons, decides, and directs an ensemble of specialized tools and models to produce video.

The LLM decomposes a complex video production request — “Create a 2-minute product commercial with three scenes, dynamic camera work, and background music” — into structured sub-tasks: scriptwriting, storyboard layout, shot-by-shot planning, per-shot generation, consistency enforcement, audio synthesis, and post-production editing. Each sub-task is routed to the best available tool or model, and the LLM coordinates the entire workflow, evaluating intermediate results and triggering re-generation when quality falls short.

💡

The Core Insight

The LLM does not generate pixels — it generates plans, decisions, and tool invocations. It acts as a cognitive layer on top of specialized visual models, much like a human film director who doesn’t personally operate every camera but orchestrates the entire production. This separation of planning intelligence from visual synthesis unlocks capabilities no single model can achieve alone: multi-scene narrative coherence, iterative quality refinement, and dynamic adaptation to user feedback.

The Paradigm Shift

Understanding the difference between traditional and agentic approaches is essential. The shift is as significant as the move from manual scripting to LLM-based code generation.

Traditional Video Generation

Single model, end-to-end. One diffusion transformer receives a text prompt and produces a video in a single forward pass (or iterative denoising loop). The user has limited control beyond prompt engineering.

  • One-shot generation — no iterative refinement
  • Limited control — camera, timing, and composition are implicit in the prompt
  • Short clips only — typically 5–10 seconds due to model context limits
  • No narrative structure — each clip is independent, no multi-scene coherence
  • Monolithic — can’t swap in a better model for specific sub-tasks
Examples: Sora, Veo 3, Wan 2.1, Kling (standalone mode)
Agentic Video Generation

Multi-model orchestration, LLM-directed. An LLM agent decomposes the task, plans the production, and coordinates specialized models for each sub-task. The user can provide high-level creative direction.

  • Iterative refinement — LLM evaluates outputs and triggers re-generation
  • Fine-grained control — explicit camera angles, shot composition, pacing
  • Long-form content — multi-minute videos with scene transitions
  • Narrative coherence — storyboard-driven, character/scene consistency
  • Composable — best-of-breed models for each stage, hot-swappable
Examples: FilmAgent, MovieAgent, LAVE, VideoAgent, Veo 3 + Gemini Flow

Why Now? The Convergence of Three Capabilities

Agentic video generation has emerged in 2024–2026 because three previously separate capabilities have simultaneously reached critical maturity:

  • Capable LLMs with tool use: Models like GPT-4o, Claude, and Gemini 2.5 can now reliably plan multi-step workflows, generate structured outputs (JSON scene descriptions, camera parameters), and invoke external tools — the prerequisite for any agentic system.
  • High-quality video generation models: Sora, Veo 3, Wan 2.1, Kling, and HunyuanVideo produce photorealistic clips that are finally good enough to serve as “raw material” for LLM-orchestrated production. Individual clips now look professional; the bottleneck has shifted to composition and coherence.
  • Mature video understanding: Vision-language models can now analyze generated clips for quality, consistency, and semantic correctness — enabling the closed-loop feedback that makes iterative refinement possible. The LLM can actually watch what it produced and decide whether to keep or redo it.

This convergence means the ceiling of what a single prompt can achieve has been shattered. The question is no longer “can AI generate a good 5-second clip?” but “can AI direct an entire production?” The answer, increasingly, is yes.

The AI Director Paradigm

Real filmmaking is a collaborative process: a director coordinates writers, actors, cinematographers, and editors, each contributing specialized skills. AI Director systems mirror this structure by assigning distinct roles to LLM agents or specialized models. The director agent maintains the narrative vision while delegating concrete subtasks — dialogue writing, shot composition, camera placement, character blocking — to focused sub-agents.

Key Insight: Decomposition Beats Monolithic Generation

A single end-to-end video model must simultaneously handle story logic, visual composition, character consistency, camera movement, and temporal coherence. AI Director systems decompose this into a pipeline of manageable subtasks, each handled by an agent optimized for that specific problem. FilmAgent demonstrated that a well-coordinated team of GPT-4o agents outperforms a single, more powerful o1 model — proving that orchestration architecture matters more than raw model capability for complex creative tasks.

AI Director Pipeline: From Script to Final Video

FilmAgent: Multi-Agent Film Crew

FilmAgent (SIGGRAPH Asia 2024 / arXiv Jan 2025) simulates a complete film crew with specialized LLM agents playing the roles of director, screenwriter, actors, and cinematographer. The system covers the full pipeline from initial concept to rendered 3D video.

1

Idea Development

Transforms brainstormed ideas into structured story outlines with scene breakdowns. The director agent sets the narrative arc while the screenwriter agent elaborates plot points.

2

Scriptwriting via Multi-Agent Discussion

Agents collaborate through iterative feedback and revision cycles to elaborate dialogue, character actions, and blocking for each scene. The director reviews drafts and requests changes; actors verify their lines feel natural; the continuity agent flags inconsistencies.

3

Cinematography Planning

The cinematographer agent determines camera setups, shot types (close-up, wide, tracking), and transitions for each shot. Decisions are grounded in the emotional tone of the scene.

4

3D Rendering

Final output is rendered in a Unity-based 3D virtual environment using pre-built character models and scene assets. This guarantees deterministic, artifact-free rendering.

⚡
Multi-agent beats monolithic: Despite using GPT-4o (a less advanced model), FilmAgent outperforms single-agent o1 across all filmmaking metrics. Human evaluation scores average 3.98/5.0 across script quality, acting naturalness, and cinematographic coherence. This demonstrates that well-coordinated multi-agent collaboration matters more than raw model power for complex creative tasks.

MovieAgent: Chain-of-Thought Movie Production

MovieAgent (March 2025, Show Lab / NUS) introduces hierarchical Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning for automated movie production. Given a script and character bank, the system employs multiple LLM agents that mirror real production roles:

Director Agent

Overall narrative planning and scene structuring. Ensures the story arc remains coherent across all scenes and maintains dramatic pacing.

Screenwriter Agent

Dialogue and action elaboration. Expands brief scene descriptions into detailed shot-level scripts with character motivations and emotional beats.

Storyboard Artist Agent

Visual composition planning. Translates script descriptions into spatial layouts, shot compositions, and visual references for each scene.

Location Manager Agent

Setting and environment selection. Determines backgrounds, lighting conditions, and environmental details that match the scene’s mood and narrative context.

The CoT planning automatically structures scenes, camera settings, and cinematography with minimal manual input. MovieAgent achieves state-of-the-art in script faithfulness, character consistency (via a pre-defined character bank), and narrative coherence, generating multi-scene long-form videos with synchronized subtitles and audio.

VideoDirectorGPT: LLM-Guided Consistent Video

VideoDirectorGPT (COLM 2024) takes a two-stage approach that separates planning from generation, using GPT-4 as a planning backbone:

Stage 1: Video Planning

GPT-4 expands a single text prompt into a structured video plan containing: scene descriptions, entity descriptions with spatial layouts, background specifications, and consistency groupings that link the same entity across scenes.

The consistency groupings are the key innovation — they explicitly tell the generation stage which entities should look the same across different scenes.

Stage 2: Layout2Vid Generation

A novel grounded generation module renders video from the plan with explicit spatial layout control and entity-level temporal consistency. Trained with only image-level annotations, avoiding expensive video-level supervision.

Substantially improves layout and movement control in both single-scene and multi-scene generation. Competitive with state-of-the-art in open-domain single-scene T2V.

DirectorLLM (Meta, BMVC 2025) fine-tunes Llama 3 to generate detailed human motion instructions — poses, bounding boxes, skeletal keypoints — from text prompts. Rather than generating pixels directly, the LLM plans the motion, and a separate renderer produces the final video.

Narrative Video: Vlogger, StoryAgent, TaleWeaver

A related family of AI Director systems focuses on long-form narrative content — vlogs, stories, and multi-modal presentations. These systems use LLMs to decompose narratives into structured multi-scene scripts and then generate each scene with character and temporal consistency.

Vlogger — "Make Your Dream A Vlog" (Jan 2024)

Four-stage LLM-directed pipeline: (1) Script — GPT-4 converts a user story into a multi-scene script with shooting durations. (2) Actor — LLM identifies characters and invokes SDXL to generate reference images. (3) ShowMaker — video generation with character and scene consistency. (4) Voicer — TTS narration aligned to scenes.

Generates over 5-minute vlogs from open-world descriptions without loss of coherence. Handles complex storylines with diversified scenes across minute-level content.

StoryAgent — Customized Storytelling Video (Nov 2024)

A multi-agent framework for Customized Storytelling Video Generation (CSVG) that assigns specialized agents for story design, storyboard generation, video creation, coordination, and result evaluation. Uses LoRA-BE (LoRA-based consistency) for enhanced intra-shot temporal consistency, plus a novel storyboard pipeline for inter-shot subject consistency.

The dedicated evaluation agent reviews generated content and triggers re-generation when quality falls below thresholds — a built-in quality control loop absent from most pipelines.

MM-StoryAgent — Multi-Modal Storybooks (March 2025)

Extends StoryAgent to full multi-modal output: video, speech narration, background music, and sound effects. LLMs coordinate diverse expert tools and generative models across modalities, producing immersive audio-visual storybook experiences. The key challenge is audio-visual synchronization — aligning generated speech with character lip movements and music with scene transitions.

Open Problems

Scaling to Feature-Length Content

Current systems handle minutes of content. Scaling to hour-long narratives requires advances in long-range coherence, memory management, and hierarchical planning that maintains consistency across hundreds of shots.

Real-World Rendering Quality

Most systems operate in virtual 3D environments or rely on diffusion-based generation. Bridging to photorealistic, production-quality rendering — especially for human faces and complex environments — remains a major gap.

Interactive Human-AI Co-Direction

Enabling human directors to collaborate with AI agents in real-time, providing mid-generation feedback and creative steering, rather than one-shot prompt-to-video workflows.

Emotional and Dramatic Understanding

LLMs can plan plot structure but struggle with nuanced emotional pacing, dramatic timing, and the visual storytelling grammar that distinguishes compelling filmmaking from mechanical shot assembly.

💡
The trajectory is clear: AI Director systems are converging toward full multi-agent hierarchies that mirror real production crews, with iterative feedback loops replacing one-pass pipelines. The remaining gaps — feature-length coherence, photorealism, and emotional nuance — are where the next generation of research will focus. The key architectural lesson: decompose the creative process into specialized roles, coordinate them with structured protocols, and iterate until quality converges.

Why Multi-Agent?

Video production involves fundamentally different skills — writing dialogue, composing shots, generating visuals, synchronizing audio, and quality-checking continuity. A single model forced to handle all of these simultaneously hits capacity limits. Multi-agent systems solve this by assigning each skill to a dedicated agent optimized for its specific role, with a coordinator ensuring coherent output across the pipeline.

Single-Agent Approach

One large model handles everything: script, visuals, audio, editing. Simpler to deploy but quality is bounded by a single model’s capacity. Struggles with multi-faceted tasks and opaque failure modes.

Limited by weakest capability

Multi-Agent Approach

Specialized agents for each production role, coordinated through structured protocols. Each agent can be independently optimized, debugged, or replaced. Quality scales with the number of specialized components.

Modular and extensible

Role-Based Agent Architecture

Multi-agent video pipelines organize agents into a hierarchy that mirrors a real film production crew. Each agent has a defined responsibility, input/output contract, and communication protocol with other agents.

Hierarchical Multi-Agent Production Architecture

Pipeline Architecture Patterns

Multi-agent video systems use four distinct architectural patterns, each with different trade-offs between quality, speed, and complexity:

1

Sequential Pipeline

Script → Storyboard → Character Design → Shot Generation → Assembly. Simple and fast but no feedback loops — errors in early stages cascade unchecked through the entire pipeline.

Used by: Vlogger, AesopAgent, basic pipelines

2

Iterative Pipeline with Feedback

Script ↔ Storyboard ↔ Generation ↔ Evaluation. Each stage can request revisions from previous stages. Quality improves through iteration, but latency increases proportionally with the number of revision cycles.

Used by: GenMAC, FilmAgent

3

Hierarchical Multi-Agent

A Director agent dispatches sub-tasks to specialized agents (Screenwriter, Cinematographer, Actor, Editor), reviews outputs, and requests refinements. Mirrors real production hierarchy with clear authority chains.

Used by: MovieAgent, Hollywood Town

4

Graph-Based Orchestration

Agents are nodes in a directed graph with dependency edges and feedback cycles. Hypergraph nodes enable group discussions among agents that lack sufficient individual context. The most sophisticated pattern, but also the most complex to implement and debug.

Used by: Hollywood Town (OmniAgent)

Key Multi-Agent Systems

Kubrick — Blender-Based Multi-Agent Video (Aug 2024)

Named after the legendary filmmaker, Kubrick uses an LLM-Director that decomposes video descriptions into sub-processes, paired with an LLM-Programmer that writes executable Python/Blender scripts for each sub-task. Blender renders the final 3D video deterministically.

Key advantage: Deterministic rendering via Blender eliminates diffusion artifacts. The output is reproducible and precisely controllable. However, visual style is constrained to CG rather than photorealistic.

A feedback loop allows the Director to evaluate rendered results and request script revisions from the Programmer.

GenMAC — Compositional T2V via Multi-Agent Collaboration (Dec 2024)

GenMAC introduces an iterative multi-agent framework with four specialized roles: Verification Agent (checks prompt faithfulness), Suggestion Agent (proposes improvements), Correction Agent (applies fixes with self-routing for adaptive selection), and Output Structuring Agent (formats results for the next iteration).

Key innovation: Self-routing mechanism allows the system to adaptively select the most suitable correction strategy for each identified issue, rather than applying a fixed correction pipeline.

Mora — Generalist Video Generation (March 2024)

Mora aims to replicate Sora-like capabilities by orchestrating existing open-source models through multi-agent coordination, rather than training a single monolithic model. An LLM coordinator manages the workflow and passes intermediate results between specialized agents.

Key advantage: No end-to-end training required — leverage existing foundation models and improve by swapping in better components. Quality is bounded by the weakest component model, and inter-agent communication adds overhead.

AesopAgent — Evolutionary Story-to-Video (March 2024)

Converts user story proposals into complete video productions through script generation, image generation, audio generation, and video composition. The key innovation is a RAG-based evolutionary system that continuously improves by accumulating expert experience, optimizing LLM prompts based on past performance, and refining tool usage over time.

Unlike static pipelines, AesopAgent gets better with use — a self-improving multi-agent system that learns from its own production history.

ViMax — Agentic Video Generation (Dec 2025)

ViMax functions as Director, Screenwriter, Producer, and Generator all-in-one. Core components: (1) RAG-based Long Script Design Engine that segments lengthy stories into multi-scene scripts, (2) Shot-Level Storyboard Design using cinematographic language, (3) Multi-Camera Filming Simulation for immersive viewing, and (4) Character Consistency Engine for visual coherence across scenes.

End-to-end from user concept to final video. Open-source. RAG-based script handling enables processing of arbitrarily long source material.

Hollywood Town — Graph-Based Orchestration (Oct 2025)

Introduces OmniAgent, a hierarchical graph-based multi-agent framework. Key innovations: (1) Hypergraph nodes enable temporary group discussions among agents lacking sufficient context, reducing individual memory requirements. (2) Directed Cyclic Graphs (DCGs) with limited retries allow agents to reflect and refine outputs iteratively through feedback from later pipeline stages.

This is the most sophisticated architecture pattern to date — feedback from later stages (e.g., visual quality assessment) can trigger revisions in earlier stages (e.g., script rewriting), with retry limits preventing infinite loops.

Open Problems

Agent Communication Protocols

Standardized interfaces between agents are needed for interoperability and plug-and-play agent replacement. Currently, each system defines its own bespoke communication format.

Quality Cascading

Errors from early pipeline stages (a bad script) cascade and amplify through later stages (poor visuals, broken continuity). Robust feedback mechanisms and early-stage quality gates are essential to prevent downstream degradation.

Computational Cost

Multi-agent pipelines require many model inferences — often dozens of LLM calls plus multiple image/video generation invocations per shot. Reducing total compute while maintaining quality is critical for practical deployment.

Memory and Context Management

As pipelines grow in complexity, managing context across many agents becomes a significant bottleneck. Each agent needs access to relevant context from other agents without exceeding token limits. Hollywood Town’s hypergraph approach — temporary group discussions for context sharing — is one early solution.

Style and Tone Consistency

Ensuring consistent artistic style and emotional tone across all pipeline outputs — from script tone to visual palette to music mood — requires cross-agent coordination that current systems handle only coarsely.

💡
Where multi-agent video production is heading: The trajectory moves from sequential pipelines (Vlogger, 2024) to iterative feedback loops (GenMAC, 2024) to full graph-based orchestration with hypergraph context sharing (Hollywood Town, 2025). The next step is standardized agent protocols that enable mix-and-match composition of agents from different research groups and commercial providers — a true ecosystem of interoperable video production agents.

1. What Is Agentic Video Editing?

Traditional video editing requires users to manually specify every cut, transition, filter, and effect through complex timeline-based software. Agentic video editing replaces this manual workflow with an LLM-powered agent that accepts natural-language commands — like “make a travel highlight reel from my Paris footage” — and autonomously plans and executes the necessary editing steps.

The agent paradigm introduces a fundamentally different interaction model: instead of the user operating tools directly, the user communicates intent, and the LLM translates that intent into a structured sequence of tool calls. This requires the agent to understand video content, decompose complex instructions, select appropriate tools, estimate parameters, execute edits, and verify quality.

Intuition: Agent vs. Instruction-Based Editing

Instruction-based editing (e.g., InstructVid2Vid) trains a single end-to-end model that maps text instructions to pixel-level modifications — like a specialist who can only do one type of operation per request. An agentic editor is more like a director with a toolbox: it reasons about what needs to happen, breaks the job into steps, picks the right tool for each step, and orchestrates the full workflow. The agent can handle multi-step, interdependent operations that no single model could execute end-to-end.

3. LAVE: LLM Agent for Video Editing

LAVE (LLM-Powered Agent Assistance and Language Augmentation for Video Editing) is a plan-and-execute agent integrated within a real video editing UI, developed at the University of Toronto and Meta Reality Labs.

LAVE — Architecture Overview

Paper: Wang et al., ACM IUI 2024  |  arXiv 2402.10294

LAVE’s key innovation is language augmentation: before reasoning about any edit, the system automatically generates rich textual descriptions of the user’s footage — captions, transcripts, and scene descriptions. This creates a language-based representation of the video that enables a text-only LLM to reason about visual content without requiring multimodal input.

1

Language Augmentation

Automatically generate captions, transcripts, and scene descriptions for all footage. This creates a textual proxy that the LLM can reason over — enabling video understanding without multimodal models.

2

Instruction Analysis & Planning

When the user provides an editing objective (e.g., “create a 60-second highlight reel”), the LLM analyzes the footage descriptions and plans a sequence of editing actions: cut, trim, arrange, add transitions, overlay text, etc.

3

Plan Review & Execution

The agent’s plan is shown to the user before execution for approval or modification. Once approved, LAVE executes the plan through the editing tool’s API — calling timeline manipulation, effects, and transition functions.

4

Iterative Refinement

Users can edit the agent’s output manually or issue follow-up commands. The hybrid interaction model allows seamless switching between agent-driven and manual editing at any time.

🔗
Evaluation finding: In a user study with 8 participants (novices to proficient editors), LAVE demonstrated effectiveness across diverse editing tasks. Key insight: users valued plan visibility — seeing the agent’s proposed steps before execution increased trust and enabled creative collaboration rather than simple delegation.

4. VideoAgent: Unified Understanding + Editing

VideoAgent (HKUDS, Hong Kong University) is a unified agentic framework that handles video understanding, editing, and remaking within a single system — eliminating the need to switch between separate tools for analysis and modification.

5. MotionAgent: Motion-Aware Editing with LLM Control

MotionAgent addresses a specific pain point in video generation and editing: precise motion control. While text prompts can describe what objects should look like, they struggle to specify how objects should move. MotionAgent uses an LLM as a motion-field agent that translates text descriptions into explicit motion representations.

MotionAgent — Fine-grained Controllable Video Generation (Feb 2025)

9. Key Challenges

Despite rapid progress, agentic video editing faces several fundamental challenges that limit practical deployment.

Temporal Consistency at Scale

Maintaining visual coherence across long edits (hours of footage) remains the hardest problem. Agent-based approaches rely on their underlying tools for frame-level consistency, but orchestrating those tools across hundreds of cuts and transitions introduces compounding errors. Even small per-frame artifacts become glaring flicker when played back at 24+ FPS.

Tool Integration Brittleness

Current agents depend on fixed tool libraries with specific API signatures. When a tool fails, returns unexpected output, or lacks a needed capability, the agent must either recover gracefully or admit failure. Robust error handling and tool fallback strategies are still immature — most systems handle the “happy path” well but struggle with edge cases.

Instruction Ambiguity

Creative editing instructions are inherently ambiguous. “Make this more dramatic” could mean darker color grading, faster cuts, more intense music, tighter close-ups, or all of the above. Agents must develop robust models of creative intent and ideally ask clarifying questions rather than guessing — a capability most current systems lack.

Complex Multi-Step Edits

Current systems handle single-step instructions well but struggle with complex, interdependent workflows (e.g., “restructure this documentary to build more tension, then add appropriate music”). Long-horizon planning with dependency tracking remains an open problem for LLM-based agents.

Multimodal Understanding Gap

Professional editing requires understanding audio, music, pacing, emotional tone, and narrative arc — not just visual content. Most current agents reason primarily about visual frames and text metadata, missing the full spectrum of signals that human editors intuitively process.

⚠

The Creative Partnership Frontier

The field is moving beyond simple command execution toward genuine creative collaboration, where agents suggest edits, offer alternatives, and understand artistic intent. This requires not just technical capability but a model of aesthetics and storytelling — arguably the hardest aspect of video editing to formalize. Early systems like LAVE’s hybrid interaction model and From Shots to Stories’ narrative-level editing point the way, but true AI co-creativity remains an open frontier.

1. The Agentic Approach to Video Understanding

Processing an entire video through a single multimodal model is fundamentally limited by context length and computational cost. A 10-minute video at 30 FPS contains 18,000 frames — far more than any model can process at once. The agentic approach solves this by using the LLM as a central reasoning engine that decides which parts of the video to examine, which tools to invoke, and how to synthesize the results.

This mirrors how a human expert watches video: you don’t stare at every frame equally. You scan for relevant moments, zoom in on details, rewatch key segments, and piece together an understanding. Video understanding agents formalize this process as a tool-use loop.

Intuition: End-to-End vs. Agentic Video Understanding

An end-to-end multimodal model (e.g., Gemini 1.5 Pro) ingests the full video and produces an answer in one forward pass — like reading an entire book before answering a question. An agentic system is like a researcher with a table of contents: it plans what to look for, jumps to the relevant sections, examines details with specialized instruments (OCR, object detection, ASR), and iterates until confident. The agent approach can match or exceed end-to-end models while processing less than 1% of frames.

The field has evolved rapidly from initial frame-selection agents to sophisticated multi-agent systems with memory, uncertainty awareness, and reinforcement-learned coordination.

The foundational system that established the agentic approach to video understanding. The LLM iteratively identifies and compiles crucial information to answer questions about long videos by: (1) planning what information is needed, (2) selecting which frames to examine, (3) invoking vision-language models as tools, (4) reasoning over gathered information, and (5) iterating if insufficient.

VideoAgent (Memory-Augmented) — Structured Memory for Video Understanding (ECCV 2024)

A separate system (same name, different paper) that reconciles multiple foundation models through a unified memory mechanism. Builds two types of memory from the video: (1) temporal event descriptions capturing what happens over time, and (2) object-centric tracking states following specific objects across frames. Given a query, the agent employs tools including segment localization, object memory querying, and visual model invocation.

6. How Agents Decompose Complex Video Questions

The power of agentic video understanding lies in task decomposition: breaking complex, multi-faceted video questions into a series of targeted sub-queries that can each be answered by a specific tool.

Example Decomposition: “What did the person in the red shirt do after picking up the box, and was anyone watching?”
1

Frame Selection

Sample frames across the video timeline. Identify the temporal range containing the relevant event.

2

Person Detection

Invoke object detection + attribute recognition to find the person wearing a red shirt across sampled frames.

3

Object & Temporal Grounding

Locate “box” in frames, find the frame where the person picks it up, and identify the temporal window after this event.

4

Action Recognition

Classify what the person does in the subsequent frames using action recognition tools (SlowFast, VideoMAE).

5

Scene Analysis & Reasoning

Check for other people in the scene and determine if they are watching. Synthesize all gathered information into a coherent natural-language answer.

Key insight: This entire decomposition — choosing which tools to call, in what order, and how to interpret results — is planned and executed by the LLM agent. Different questions about the same video will produce entirely different tool-use plans. This flexibility is what makes agentic approaches superior to fixed pipelines for open-ended video QA.

8. Open Problems and Future Directions

Streaming Video Understanding

Current agents operate on pre-recorded video. A major frontier is agents that can understand live or streaming video in real-time, maintaining state as new content arrives — essential for applications like surveillance, live sports analysis, and real-time captioning.

Multi-Video Reasoning

Agents that can reason across multiple videos simultaneously (e.g., “compare the cooking techniques in these two videos”) require cross-video memory and alignment capabilities that current systems lack.

Causal and Counterfactual Reasoning

Understanding not just what happened but why, and what might have happened differently. Current agents excel at descriptive queries but struggle with causal and hypothetical reasoning about video events.

Hallucination Reduction

Video understanding agents can hallucinate details not present in the video — a problem inherited from their LLM backbones. Grounding mechanisms and uncertainty-aware approaches (VideoAgent2) help but do not fully solve this. Robust visual grounding and verification loops remain critical needs.

Audio-Visual Integration

Better integration of audio understanding (dialogue, music, ambient sounds) with visual analysis. Most current agents treat audio as an afterthought (ASR transcripts only), missing rich information encoded in tone, music, and sound effects.

Efficiency at Scale

Processing hour-long or day-long video streams efficiently without excessive compute or storage. While agents are already more frame-efficient than brute-force approaches, the overhead of iterative tool calling and LLM reasoning adds up for very long content.

⚠

The Evaluation Gap

Current benchmarks (EgoSchema, NExT-QA, MLVU) test final answer accuracy but not agent efficiency, tool usage patterns, or reasoning quality. An agent that gets the right answer using 3 tool calls is fundamentally better than one using 30, but current metrics treat them as equivalent. Developing standardized evaluation frameworks that capture the full picture of agentic performance — accuracy, efficiency, robustness, and reasoning quality — is an important open problem.

World Models

From passive video generation to action-conditioned simulation: world models learn to predict the future, enabling agents to plan, explore, and train inside generated environments.

1. What Are World Models?

A world model is a neural network that predicts the next observation given the current state and an action. Where standard video generation produces a predetermined clip, a world model closes the loop: the agent (or human) observes the generated frame, takes an action, and the model produces the next frame accordingly — creating a real-time interactive simulation.

The concept dates back to Ha & Schmidhuber (2018), who showed that a compact VAE + LSTM world model could support agent planning entirely “in imagination.” Modern video diffusion models bring this idea to photorealistic fidelity, transforming it from academic curiosity into practical infrastructure for games, robotics, and autonomous driving.

Intuition: Why World Models Matter for Agentic Video

An agentic video system does not just generate video — it responds to actions and decisions. World models provide the core capability: given “what has happened” and “what the agent does next,” predict “what will happen.” This action-conditioned loop is what separates a movie projector from a game engine — and it is the foundation for any system where an AI agent or human user steers video generation in real time.

The World Model Loop
💡
The key constraint: For the loop to feel interactive, the world model must generate each frame in under 50ms (20+ FPS). Standard video diffusion takes minutes per clip — making the world model loop 1000× too slow. Every system below tackles this latency challenge differently: custom ASICs (Oasis), causal attention + KV caching (LongLive), single-step diffusion (GameNGen), or massive cloud infrastructure (Genie 3).

2. Key Systems

Genie 2 (Google DeepMind, December 2024)

A large-scale foundation world model that generates action-controllable, playable 3D environments from a single prompt image. A massive leap beyond Genie 1’s 256×256 2D platformers:

  • 3D environments with first-person and isometric viewpoints — photorealistic indoor/outdoor scenes, driving environments, fantasy worlds
  • Playable by humans or AI agents via keyboard and mouse input
  • Consistent generation for 10–60 seconds with coherent world state, object permanence, and proper perspective
  • Diverse environment generation — produces an endless variety of worlds from a single seed image

Primary use: Training and evaluating embodied AI agents in controlled, diverse environments. Used internally at DeepMind as a rapid prototyping platform.

Limitations: Visual artifacts accumulate after ~60 seconds; not publicly available during initial release period.

Genie 3 / Project Genie (Google DeepMind, August 2025 — Public January 2026)

The first world model achieving real-time HD interaction. A generational leap that makes interactive world generation publicly accessible:

  • 720p resolution at 24 FPS — real-time generation with no perceptible lag
  • Text-to-world generation — creates dynamic, navigable environments from text descriptions alone (no seed image required)
  • Multi-minute consistency — retains environmental coherence for several minutes of continuous exploration, a major improvement over Genie 2’s ~60s limit
  • World remixing — generated worlds can be explored, then modified via new prompts to create variations

Availability: Rolled out through Google Labs as Project Genie in late January 2026, available to Google One AI Ultra subscribers in the US.

Limitations: Physics are approximated rather than simulated; generation is cloud-based (not on-device); limited geographic availability during rollout.

DIAMOND — DIffusion As a Model Of eNvironment Dreams (NeurIPS 2024 Spotlight)

Diffusion models as world models for reinforcement learning. DIAMOND challenges the dominant paradigm of using discrete latent tokens for world modeling, arguing that token-based compression loses the visual details that RL agents need:

  • Approach: A diffusion model predicts the next frame conditioned on past frames and agent actions. An RL agent is trained entirely inside this diffusion world model (“in dreams”)
  • Atari 100k benchmark: Mean human-normalized score of 1.46 (46% better than humans) — the best result for agents trained in world models on 100K environment frames
  • CS:GO demonstration: Trained to simulate Counter-Strike: Global Offensive; playable at ~10 FPS on an RTX 3090

Key insight: Diffusion models produce sharper frames with more accurate fine-grained detail (exact score displays, precise object positions) than VAE/GAN world models. This visual fidelity translates directly to better RL training signal. PSNR gap: 29+ dB (diffusion) vs. ~24 dB (VAE).

Open source: Yes — model weights and code publicly available.

Oasis (Decart AI, October 2024)

Real-time Minecraft-like world generation at 20 FPS. No game engine, no hand-coded physics, no 3D assets — everything is pure neural rendering from keyboard and mouse inputs:

  • Architecture: Transformer-based next-frame prediction with a DiT backbone + ViT autoencoder
  • Speed: 20 FPS real-time generation — each frame in ~40ms, 100× faster than standard text-to-video models
  • Training data: Millions of hours of Minecraft gameplay footage
  • Hardware: Optimized for Etched Sohu custom Transformer ASIC for maximum throughput

Emergent understanding: The model learned complex Minecraft mechanics purely from video observation — building, lighting physics, inventory management, and object interactions — with no explicit rules or code.

Open source: 500M parameter model weights and code publicly released. Larger model available as a playable browser demo.

Limitations: No persistent memory — scenery and inventory change unpredictably; consistency degrades over extended sessions.

NVIDIA Cosmos (January 2025 — ongoing)

World foundation models for Physical AI. A family of models designed specifically for physics-aware simulation, targeting robotics, autonomous driving, and industrial automation:

  • Cosmos-Predict1: General-purpose world foundation models for fine-tuning into task-specific world models
  • Cosmos-Predict2.5: Specialized for predicting future world states in video form
  • Cosmos-Transfer2.5: Produces world simulations conditioned on multiple spatial control inputs
  • Multimodal input: Accepts text, image, video, and movement data
  • Physics-awareness: Accurately models spatial relationships and physical interactions

Target applications: Generating diverse driving scenarios for autonomous vehicle training, robotic manipulation policy training, industrial automation simulation, and synthetic training data at scale.

Open source: Open-weight with permissive license. Enterprise-grade platform with strong NVIDIA ecosystem integration.

Limitations: Primarily designed for Physical AI rather than entertainment; requires significant multi-GPU compute; offline generation (not real-time interactive).

GameNGen (Google Research, August 2024 — ICLR 2025)

The first game engine powered entirely by a neural model. Demonstrated on DOOM — the classic first-person shooter runs at real-time speeds with no traditional game engine:

  • Two-phase training: (1) an RL agent learns to play DOOM and records gameplay sessions, then (2) an augmented Stable Diffusion model learns to predict next frames from these action-frame sequences
  • Speed: 20 FPS on a single TPU
  • Quality: Next-frame PSNR of 29.4 dB (comparable to lossy JPEG compression)
  • Stability: Remains stable over 5+ minutes of autoregressive generation
  • Human evaluation: Raters could barely distinguish real DOOM gameplay from generated frames (near-random accuracy)

Limitations: Requires RL agent to generate training data; limited to the single game it was trained on; no world modification capability.

UniSim — Universal Simulator (ICLR 2024)

A universal simulator of real-world interaction learned through generative modeling. UniSim unifies diverse data sources into a single action-conditioned video generation model:

  • Universal action interface: Handles motor controls (robotic actuators), language descriptions (text commands), and camera motions — all through a single model
  • Data fusion: Combines object-rich image data, densely-sampled robotics action sequences, and diverse navigation movements into a unified training pipeline
  • Variable-length prediction: Generates the next set of observation frames given past observations and any supported action type

Key results: Trained high-level vision-language planners and low-level RL policies that transferred to real-world robotic tasks with zero-shot success — no fine-tuning on real data needed. Bridges the simulation-to-reality gap through data diversity.

Limitations: Resolution and visual quality below specialized models; action space may not cover all real-world interaction types.

4. Key Challenges

Despite rapid progress from Genie 1’s 1 FPS (February 2024) to Genie 3’s 24 FPS at 720p (January 2026), fundamental challenges remain unsolved:

1

Real-Time Generation at Scale

The latency budget is brutal: ~40–50ms per frame for 20–24 FPS interaction. This constrains model size, denoising steps, and resolution simultaneously. Current solutions — custom ASICs (Oasis/Etched), aggressive step reduction, KV caching — achieve real-time at modest resolutions, but 1080p at 60 FPS (the gaming standard) remains orders of magnitude beyond current capability.

2

Physics Grounding

Most world models learn approximate physics from statistical patterns in video data. Objects may float, interpenetrate, or defy gravity in edge cases. Cosmos takes the most explicit approach to physics awareness, while others rely on emergent physical behavior. The gap between “looks physically plausible” and “is physically correct” matters enormously for robotics and driving applications where wrong physics = wrong policies.

3

Long-Horizon Consistency

All systems face drift — small errors accumulate frame-by-frame, leading to visual artifacts, geometry violations, and loss of scene coherence. Genie 3 achieves multi-minute consistency (a major milestone), GameNGen remains stable for 5+ minutes through augmentation-based training, and Oasis accepts inconsistency as an explicit trade-off for real-time speed. But hour-scale consistency remains unsolved.

4

Memory and Persistent State

Without explicit memory, generated worlds lack object permanence — objects leaving the viewport may vanish or transform when they re-enter. Inventories change randomly (Oasis), rooms rearrange upon revisiting (Genie). The autoregressive frame-by-frame paradigm has no persistent world state; everything must be reconstructed from a limited context window. Research directions include external memory banks, state-tracking modules, and hybrid neural-symbolic architectures.

⚠

The Fundamental Tension

Traditional game engines are exact simulators — every physics calculation, collision check, and state update is deterministic and correct. Neural world models are approximate simulators — they produce statistically plausible outputs but offer no correctness guarantees. The field is converging on hybrid approaches: neural rendering for visual fidelity combined with explicit state tracking and physics constraints for correctness. This hybrid architecture may be the path to truly reliable world models.

1. The Real-Time Challenge

Standard video diffusion models take minutes per clip. Interactive applications need frames in under 50 milliseconds. That is a 1000× gap — and closing it requires rethinking the entire diffusion pipeline, from architecture to hardware.

The definition of “real-time” depends on the application. A live game requires 30–60 FPS with imperceptible input latency. A live-stream style transfer can tolerate 15–24 FPS. An interactive editing tool may work at 5–15 FPS. Each tier imposes different constraints on model size, denoising steps, and hardware requirements.

💡
Why this matters for agentic video: An agentic system that steers video generation — whether driven by an AI agent or a human user — requires real-time feedback. If the model takes 30 seconds to produce a clip, the agent cannot react, iterate, or adapt. Real-time generation is the enabling technology that turns passive video generation into interactive, steerable media.

3. Key Systems

StreamDiffusion (ICCV/ICLR 2025)

A pipeline-level solution for real-time interactive generation. StreamDiffusion reimagines the diffusion pipeline to eliminate sequential bottlenecks through three key innovations:

  • Stream Batch: Instead of completing all denoising steps for one image before starting the next, Stream Batch processes multiple images at different denoising stages simultaneously. While image N is at step 5 of denoising, image N+1 is at step 4, N+2 at step 3, and so on. Result: 1.5× higher throughput vs. conventional sequential denoising
  • Residual Classifier-Free Guidance (RCFG): Standard CFG doubles compute by requiring both positive and negative conditional passes. RCFG computes residuals rather than full negative passes, achieving up to 2.05× speedup over conventional CFG with minimal quality loss
  • Pipeline-Level Optimization: Input pre-processing, model inference, and post-processing are pipelined across CPU and GPU to maximize hardware utilization

Performance: Up to 91.07 FPS for image generation on a single RTX 4090 — 59.6× faster than Diffusers Autopipeline.

Key trade-off: StreamDiffusion operates on individual images (not native video frames), so temporal coherence must be maintained through ControlNet conditioning or frame-to-frame latent blending. It is a drop-in compatible acceleration layer for existing Stable Diffusion models.

Open source: Yes — fully available on GitHub.

StreamDiffusionV2 (MLSys 2026)

Multi-GPU scaling for production real-time generation. A major upgrade focused on scalability, quality, and deployment flexibility:

  • Multi-GPU support: Scales across diverse GPU setups, distributing pipeline stages across devices
  • Flexible denoising steps: Adjustable quality-speed trade-off at runtime — more steps for higher quality, fewer for maximum speed
  • No TensorRT or quantization required: Achieves high FPS without specialized compilation or precision reduction

Performance on 4× H100 GPUs (without TensorRT):

  • 14B-parameter model: 58.28 FPS (first frame in 0.5s)
  • 1.3B-parameter model: 64.52 FPS
  • With additional denoising steps for higher quality: 31.62 FPS (14B), 61.58 FPS (1.3B)

Significance: First system to achieve real-time FPS with a 14B-parameter model — proving that very large models can serve interactive workloads with proper parallelization.

Open source: Yes.

MirageLSD — Infinite Stable Live-Stream Diffusion (Decart AI, 2025)

The first model designed specifically for infinite, stable live-stream video generation. Where standard autoregressive approaches collapse after minutes of generation (drift accumulation), MirageLSD maintains stability indefinitely:

  • History augmentation: During fine-tuning, the model is trained on corrupted input history frames — teaching it to anticipate and correct the drift artifacts that naturally occur in autoregressive generation
  • Anti-collapse mechanism: By learning to “clean up” accumulated errors rather than propagate them, the model generates video infinitely without the visual degradation that plagues other approaches
  • Promptable: Responds to text prompts while maintaining visual consistency — users can steer the stream’s content in real time

Key innovation: History augmentation is a training-time solution to the fundamental challenge of autoregressive generation. Instead of trying to prevent errors from occurring (impossible in practice), the model learns to tolerate and correct them. This is conceptually similar to how noise-robust ASR models are trained on corrupted audio.

Open source: No — proprietary Decart AI system.

LongLive — Real-Time Interactive Long Video (September 2025)

True real-time interactive video generation (not just image streaming) at 20.7 FPS. LongLive addresses the fundamental architectural limitation that makes standard video diffusion models incompatible with real-time generation:

  • The problem: Standard video diffusion uses bidirectional attention — every frame attends to every other frame. This requires the entire sequence to be processed as a whole, preventing incremental generation
  • Causal architecture: LongLive replaces bidirectional attention with causal attention (as in LLMs), enabling KV caching and frame-by-frame incremental generation. Each new frame only attends to previous frames, never future ones
  • KV-Recache: Instead of naive KV-cache that grows linearly with sequence length, periodically re-encodes a compressed context window — bounding memory while preserving long-range information
  • Frame Sink Token: A special token accumulates scene-level context across the entire session, preventing “scene forgetting” during long interactions

Performance: 1.3B parameters, 20.7 FPS on a single GPU. Maintains prompt compliance, smooth transitions, and visual consistency over long sequences. Users can steer the narrative during generation via streaming text prompts.

Trade-off: Causal attention may sacrifice some global coherence compared to bidirectional models — future frames cannot “look ahead” to maintain consistency with upcoming events. But for interactive use, this trade-off is inherent: you cannot attend to frames that have not been generated yet.

StreamDiT — Real-Time Streaming Text-to-Video (July 2025)

Streaming text-to-video generation built natively on the DiT architecture. While StreamDiffusion accelerates image-by-image generation, StreamDiT restructures DiT inference for true streaming video output:

  • Architecture: Applies causal/streaming principles to the Diffusion Transformer (DiT) architecture, which is the backbone of most modern video generators (Sora, Wan, CogVideoX)
  • Native video: Generates temporally coherent video frames, not independent images assembled into a sequence
  • Streaming output: Frames are produced incrementally, enabling display as they are generated rather than waiting for the entire clip

Significance: Bridges the gap between StreamDiffusion (fast but image-based) and LongLive (video-native but research-stage), applying streaming optimizations to the most widely adopted video generation architecture.

Status: Early research — performance benchmarks are still emerging, but the architectural approach is sound and aligns with the field’s trajectory toward DiT-native streaming.

5. How Real-Time Solutions Stack

No single technique delivers real-time performance alone. The path to interactive generation requires stacking complementary optimizations across the entire pipeline:

Real-Time Optimization Stack
Architectural: Causal Attention + KV Caching

Replace bidirectional attention with causal (LLM-style) attention for incremental generation. Cache key-value pairs across frames. Enables streaming output. (LongLive, StreamDiT)

Pipeline: Stream Batching + RCFG

Process multiple images at different denoising stages simultaneously. Reduce CFG overhead via residual computation. 1.5–2× throughput gain. (StreamDiffusion)

Step Reduction: Distillation + Rectified Flow

Reduce denoising steps from 50–100 down to 1–8 via consistency distillation, adversarial distillation, or rectified flow training. 10–50× speedup.

Hardware: Multi-GPU + Custom ASICs + Quantization

Distribute across multiple GPUs (StreamDiffusionV2), use custom Transformer ASICs (Oasis/Etched), or apply INT8/FP8 quantization. 2–10× additional speedup.

🔗
Combined effect: Stacking causal attention + step distillation (4 steps) + stream batching + INT8 quantization can yield 250–1000× total speedup over naive video diffusion. This moves generation from minutes per clip to frames per second — the territory where interactive applications become feasible.

6. User-in-the-Loop Generation

Real-time generation enables a fundamentally new interaction paradigm: the user steers the generation process as it unfolds, rather than specifying everything upfront and waiting for a complete result.

Interactive Prompting

Changing text prompts mid-generation to steer content in real time. Supported by Genie 3, MirageLSD, and LongLive. The model blends the new prompt with its current visual state, creating smooth transitions between narrative beats.

Control Signal Injection

Real-time input via keyboard/mouse (Genie 2/3, Oasis, GameNGen), sketch/drawing input (ControlNet-based), audio/voice commands, or gesture tracking via webcam. Each modality maps to a conditioning channel in the model.

Latent Space Navigation

Directly manipulating the latent representation during generation to control specific attributes: style, motion intensity, camera angle, lighting. Provides fine-grained control without requiring natural language descriptions.

Feedback-Based Refinement

The user evaluates partial output and provides corrections that influence subsequent frames. “More dramatic lighting” or “slow down the camera” mid-generation. Requires the model to gracefully blend corrections into an ongoing sequence.

⚠

Challenges of User-in-the-Loop

Steering generation mid-stream introduces unique challenges: (1) coherence maintenance when the user changes direction abruptly, (2) balancing user control with model priors — too much user control produces visual noise, too little feels unresponsive, and (3) input-to-output latency must be below ~100ms for the interaction to feel direct rather than sluggish. These are HCI challenges as much as ML challenges.

10. Open Problems

True Real-Time Native Video at 30+ FPS

Achieving 30+ FPS native video generation (not frame-by-frame image assembly) on consumer hardware remains a major challenge. Current approaches achieve 20 FPS on datacenter GPUs or custom ASICs. The gap to consumer-grade, 60 FPS generation represents the next performance frontier.

Bidirectional vs. Causal Quality Trade-off

Causal architectures enable streaming but sacrifice global coherence — future frames cannot inform earlier ones. Hybrid approaches that combine local causal generation with periodic global refinement passes are a promising research direction.

On-Device Video Generation

Moving video generation from cloud to mobile and edge devices for privacy, latency, and offline capability. Current mobile hardware supports image generation but video remains cloud-dependent. Closing the 10–30× compute gap requires both hardware scaling and aggressive model compression.

XR-Native Generation Models

Models designed from the ground up for XR: stereo or light-field output, depth-aware generation, head-tracking integration, and 3D consistency across viewpoints. No current system meets all of these requirements simultaneously.

Multi-User Interactive Worlds

Scaling interactive generation to support multiple simultaneous users in shared environments requires consistent world state management, conflict resolution when users interact with the same objects, and synchronized generation across clients.

Real-Time Safety and Content Control

Real-time generation makes content moderation significantly harder — output cannot be pre-screened before display. Developing safety mechanisms that operate within the generation latency budget (10–50ms) without degrading quality or speed is an unsolved challenge.

The road from impressive demos to production-grade agentic video systems is long. These are the hard problems that remain — ranked by severity and impact — and the frontier research directions that may solve them.

The Gap Between Demos and Production

Agentic video generation has achieved remarkable results in controlled settings: FilmAgent outperforming single-agent o1 on filmmaking tasks, Genie 3 generating playable worlds at 24 FPS, and multi-agent pipelines producing minutes of coherent narrative video. But deploying these systems reliably at scale surfaces fundamental challenges that no amount of prompt engineering can fix.

The following problems are ranked by severity — high means the problem blocks production deployment today, medium means it limits quality or efficiency, and low means it is an emerging concern for the next generation of systems.

High-Severity Challenges

Temporal Consistency Across Long Videos

Every autoregressive generation system drifts over time. Small per-frame errors accumulate into visible artifacts: geometry warping, color shifts, objects morphing. Genie 3 maintains coherence for minutes; most systems degrade after seconds. For feature-length content, consistency must hold across hours — a 100x improvement over current state-of-the-art. The core issue is architectural: diffusion models lack persistent state representations, so they cannot track what should remain unchanged across frames.

Current best: Genie 3 (multi-minute), GameNGen (5+ min with augmentation), MirageLSD (infinite with history augmentation).

Real-Time Generation on Consumer Hardware

Interactive agentic video requires 20+ FPS with sub-50ms latency per frame. Today, this demands cloud GPUs (4x H100 for StreamDiffusionV2) or custom ASICs (Etched Sohu for Oasis). Consumer hardware — a laptop with an RTX 4060 or a flagship phone — cannot run native video generation in real time. Mobile devices handle text-to-image in 2–3 seconds but video generation remains cloud-dependent. Until this gap closes, agentic video stays tethered to the data center.

Key bottleneck: A 14B-parameter DiT model needs 4x H100 GPUs for 58 FPS. Consumer hardware is 10–50x underpowered.

Character & Identity Consistency Across Scenes

Multi-shot video requires the same character to look identical across different camera angles, lighting conditions, and scenes. Current approaches — IP-Adapter, LoRA fine-tuning, character banks — achieve partial consistency but fail on fine details: accessories disappear, clothing textures change, faces subtly morph between cuts. FilmAgent and MovieAgent mitigate this with explicit character banks, but the fundamental challenge remains: diffusion models have no persistent identity state.

Root cause: Each denoising step optimizes for local plausibility, not global identity coherence. The model has no “memory” of who a character is.

Safety & Content Moderation at Generation Time

Real-time generation makes pre-screening impossible — content is consumed as it is produced. Agentic systems are especially challenging because multiple agents interact unpredictably: a script agent may produce benign text that, combined with a visual agent’s interpretation, generates harmful content. Deepfake potential, misinformation risks, and regulatory compliance (advertising standards, medical accuracy, privacy laws) all compound. No robust real-time safety layer exists today.

Emerging approaches: Classifier-based safety heads, latent-space content filters, and post-generation watermarking (C2PA).

Medium-Severity Challenges

Physics Grounding & Realism

Video models learn what physics looks like from training data but not how physics works. Objects interpenetrate, liquids defy fluid dynamics, gravity reverses mid-scene. Benchmarks like PhysBench and VideoPhy show frontier models scoring 30–60% on basic physics, well below human-level (95%+). NVIDIA Cosmos targets physics-aware generation, but general-purpose physics grounding in diffusion models remains unsolved.

Worst domains: Fluid dynamics and state changes (<35%). Best: optics and shadows (60%+).

Multi-Agent Coordination Overhead

FilmAgent demonstrated that multi-agent GPT-4o outperforms single-agent o1, but this advantage comes at a cost: multiple model inferences, inter-agent communication, and iterative feedback loops multiply latency and compute. Hollywood Town’s hypergraph architecture addresses context management, but total inference cost remains 3–10x higher than single-agent approaches. Efficient agent communication protocols and shared context mechanisms are needed.

Tradeoff: Quality gains vs. 3–10x compute cost. GenMAC’s iterative refinement adds further overhead per correction cycle.

Energy Efficiency & Sustainability

Generating personalized video at scale is computationally expensive. A single multi-agent pipeline producing one minute of video may require hundreds of GPU-seconds across multiple models. Multiply by millions of personalized variants (as in marketing or sports applications), and the energy footprint becomes a serious environmental concern. Efficient generation methods — distillation, quantization, speculative decoding — help but do not solve the fundamental scaling problem.

Context: TurboDiffusion claims 100–200x acceleration, but real-time always-on applications still strain power budgets.

Evaluation Metrics for Agentic Video Quality

Traditional metrics (PSNR, SSIM, FID, FVD) measure low-level visual quality but miss what matters in agentic video: narrative coherence, character consistency across shots, physics plausibility, emotional pacing, and educational effectiveness. Human evaluation remains the gold standard but does not scale. Automated metrics for complex, multi-dimensional video quality are critically underdeveloped.

Gaps: No automated metric for: narrative coherence, cinematographic quality, emotional impact, or domain-specific accuracy.

Emerging Concerns

Domain Adaptation Without Retraining

Agent-based pipelines encode domain expertise in individual agents, but adapting a general-purpose pipeline to a new domain (e.g., from entertainment to legal video or medical training) still requires significant prompt engineering, tool integration, and often fine-tuning. True plug-and-play domain adaptation — swapping a "medical knowledge agent" for a "legal knowledge agent" without touching the rest of the pipeline — is an aspiration, not yet a reality.

Cultural Sensitivity in Automated Content

Agents generating video for global audiences must understand and respect cultural differences in visual communication, symbolism, narrative conventions, and taboos. A gesture that is positive in one culture may be offensive in another. Color symbolism varies dramatically. Agent-based personalization amplifies this problem: personalized content for millions of viewers across cultures requires cultural awareness at scale, which current LLMs only partially possess.

⚠
The compounding problem: These challenges do not exist in isolation. Physics failures break character consistency. Coordination overhead limits real-time generation. Safety constraints add latency. Evaluation gaps mean we cannot reliably measure progress on any of the above. Solving agentic video generation requires addressing these problems jointly, not in sequence.

Future Directions

Despite these challenges, the trajectory is clear. Here are the frontier research directions most likely to define agentic video generation over the next 2–3 years.

Persistent World State & Memory

Moving beyond stateless frame generation to models with explicit memory of objects, characters, and scene state. Hybrid neural-symbolic approaches that combine learned generation with structured state tracking (inventory systems, character databases, scene graphs) are the most promising path to hour-scale consistency. The Hollywood Town hypergraph architecture is an early step in this direction.

Causal + Bidirectional Hybrid Architectures

LongLive and StreamDiT showed that causal attention enables real-time streaming, but at the cost of global coherence. The next generation will combine local causal generation (for low latency) with periodic global refinement passes (for consistency). This mirrors how humans perceive video: we process frames sequentially but periodically “check” the overall scene for coherence.

On-Device and Edge-Native Generation

Model distillation (few-step or single-step generation), INT4/INT8 quantization, and mobile-specific architectures will progressively close the gap between cloud and consumer hardware. The CVPR 2025 EDGE workshop is driving this research. Target: interactive video generation on a flagship phone by 2028, enabling offline agentic video editing and personalized content without cloud dependency.

Standardized Agent Communication Protocols

Today, every multi-agent video system uses ad hoc communication between agents. Standardized interfaces — defining what a "storyboard" or "shot plan" or "character reference" looks like in machine-readable format — would enable plug-and-play agent replacement. Imagine swapping FilmAgent’s cinematographer for MovieAgent’s without modifying either system. This interoperability could accelerate progress across the entire field.

Compositional Physics Integration

Rather than hoping diffusion models learn physics from data alone, future systems will compose learned visual generation with explicit physics engines. NVIDIA Cosmos points the way: physics-aware world foundation models that accurately model spatial relationships and physical interactions. The endgame is generation that looks photorealistic and respects conservation laws.

Real-Time Safety and Ethical Guardrails

Content moderation must move from post-generation screening to generation-time enforcement. This means embedding safety constraints into the diffusion process itself (e.g., guided sampling away from harmful content), training dedicated safety agents that operate in parallel with generation agents, and developing real-time watermarking that survives compression and re-encoding. Regulatory frameworks (EU AI Act, US executive orders) will accelerate this.

Human-AI Co-Direction

The most impactful near-term application is not fully autonomous video generation but human-AI collaboration. Directors provide high-level creative vision; agents handle execution. The key challenge: enabling human directors to intervene at any pipeline stage without breaking the agent workflow, providing mid-generation feedback that the system incorporates seamlessly. LAVE’s hybrid interaction model is an early prototype.

Feature-Length Narrative Generation

Current systems produce minutes of content; the goal is TV episodes and feature films. This requires advances in long-range narrative planning (maintaining plot threads over hours), visual memory (character and location consistency across thousands of shots), and hierarchical generation (scene-level, sequence-level, and act-level coherence). The FilmAgent / MovieAgent pattern of decomposing filmmaking into specialized roles is the right framework, but each role must scale to vastly longer timescales.

Multi-User Interactive Worlds

Genie 3 and Oasis support single-user interaction. The next frontier is shared world models where multiple agents or humans interact simultaneously within the same generated environment. This requires consistent world state management, conflict resolution (two users modifying the same area), and bandwidth-efficient streaming of generated content to multiple clients. The implications for gaming, education, and collaborative design are enormous.

💡
The meta-trend: Agentic video generation is evolving from “make me a video” toward “be my creative collaborator.” The systems that succeed will not be the ones that generate the most photorealistic pixels, but the ones that best understand intent, iterate on feedback, and coordinate complex creative workflows — exactly the capabilities that multi-agent architectures are designed to provide.
